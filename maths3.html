Unit 3 · Session 29Foundations·

Population, sample, sampling variation & types of population

Why statistics exists, the two definitions everything rests on, and the difference between a tangible and a conceptual population.

In one line: statistics makes inferences about a whole population by studying a small random sample — and randomness is what makes the resulting uncertainty mathematically describable.

1. Why statistics at all?

Scientific measurements are subject to random variation: repeat the same measurement and you get slightly different answers. Statistics is the field concerned with the collection, analysis and interpretation of uncertain data. It lets engineers design valid experiments and draw reliable conclusions from them.

Definition

Statistics is the field of study concerned with the collection, analysis and interpretation of uncertain data.

2. The steel-rod example — the whole unit in miniature

A machine makes steel rods specified as 0.45 ± 0.02 cm. In the last hour it made 1000 rods. The quality engineer cannot measure all of them, so he draws a random sample of 50 rods and finds 46 (92%) meet the specification.

The sample proportion (92%) will almost certainly not equal the population proportion. The engineer needs to know how large that difference is likely to be. Three questions follow, and they map onto the whole course:

Definition

Inferential statistics is the set of methods used to draw conclusions about a population from sample data.

3. Population and sample

Population
the entire collection of objects or outcomes about which information is sought
In the rod example: all 1000 rods made in the last hour.
Sample
a subset of a population — the items actually observed
In the rod example: the 50 measured rods.
Simple random sample (SRS) of size n

A sample chosen by a method in which every collection of n population items is equally likely to make up the sample — exactly like a lottery.

The lottery analogy. With 10,000 tickets and 5 winners, put all tickets in a drum, mix, and draw 5. Every ticket is equally likely to be drawn, but more importantly every possible group of 5 tickets is equally likely. That second condition is the definition. A computer random-number generator reproduces the lottery: number the population 1…N, generate random integers, select the corresponding items.

4. Sampling variation

“Simple random samples always differ from their populations in some ways, and occasionally may be substantially different. Two different samples from the same population will differ from each other as well. This phenomenon is known as sampling variation.”

Navidi, Section 1.1

So why sample randomly at all? Because a random sample has no systematic mechanism tending to make it unrepresentative. Its differences from the population are due entirely to random variation, and random variation has a well-understood mathematical theory. A non-random sample may differ for unknown systematic reasons, and no theory is available to correct for them.

Examiner's point

Randomness buys analyzability, not accuracy. A random sample is still imperfect; it is simply imperfect in a way we can quantify.

Definition — conceptual population

A simple random sample may consist of values obtained from a process under identical experimental conditions. The population then consists of all the values that might possibly have been observed.

6. Worked examples from the textbook

Example 1.1 — the professor and the fitness test

A professor has 20,000 students, numbers them 1–20,000, generates 100 random integers and invites those students. Is this a simple random sample?

Yes. It is the lottery: each student has a ticket and 100 tickets are drawn. Every group of 100 students is equally likely.

Example 1.3 — the inspector who claimed “exactly 85%”

A sample of 40 bolts finds 34 good (85%). The inspector concludes exactly 85% of the shipment is good; his supervisor says the true proportion is likely close to 85% but not exactly 85%.

The supervisor is right. Sampling variation means a sample does not reflect its population perfectly. It is appropriate to say the population proportion is likely close to 85%, not that it equals 85%.

Example 1.4 — two inspectors disagree (85% vs 90%)

A second sample of 40 gives 36 good (90%). The first inspector claims the second did something wrong. No. Two different samples from the same population differ from each other and from the population — this is sampling variation, not error.

Example 1.5 — weighing a rock repeatedly

The readings can be treated as a simple random sample if the scale's physical characteristics remain the same for every weighing (identical conditions). The population is conceptual: all readings the scale could in principle produce.

Example 1.6 — a chemical process run 50 times

The population is conceptual: all yields the process will ever produce. The first 50 yields may be treated as an SRS only if they are generated under identical conditions and do not differ systematically from future runs.

When it fails: if high-yield runs tend to be followed by low-yield runs, or if yields improve over time as engineers learn, conditions are not identical and the sample is not an SRS.

Example 1.7 — morning runs only (a frame failure)

The process is run 10 times each morning for five mornings; no pattern appears. If production will run 7 a.m.–5 p.m., the population includes afternoon runs. The sample contains only morning runs, so it is not an SRS — ambient temperature, for example, may differ.

If the process will always run in the morning, the population is morning runs only, and the sample may reasonably be treated as an SRS.

The point students miss

Specify the population before deciding whether a sample is random. A plot showing no pattern is helpful evidence but never sufficient; knowledge of the process that generated the data outranks the plot.

7. Detecting a non-random sample

Plot the observations in the order they were taken. A simple random sample should show no obvious pattern or trend. An oscillatory pattern or an increasing trend suggests conditions were changing, so the data are not an SRS.

8. Key takeaways

  • Statistics exists because data are uncertain; inference goes from sample to population.
  • Population = everything of interest; sample = the part observed. Which is which depends on the question.
  • An SRS is defined by the method of selection, not by the outcome: every collection of n items must be equally likely.
  • Sampling variation is unavoidable; randomness makes it analyzable.
  • Tangible populations are finite physical objects; conceptual populations are all values a process could produce.
  • Whether data are an SRS depends on understanding the generating process, not just on the numbers.
Unit 3 · Session 30Sampling·

Sampling methods: probability vs non-probability

Simple random, systematic, stratified, cluster, convenience, judgemental, quota and snowball sampling — with the distinctions examiners test most often.

In one line: the sampling method decides whether sampling error can be estimated at all. Probability methods allow it; non-probability methods do not.

1. Why the method matters

The selected sample must be representative of the population. A non-random sample will probably be biased in some way, and the data may not represent the population. There are many ways to select a sample — some good, some bad. The textbook's concrete failure: to study student heights, choosing students from the football and basketball rosters would obviously misrepresent the height distribution; even a “convenient” sample such as everyone in one dorm may be misleading in ways nobody anticipated.

Definition — sampling method

In a statistical study, the sampling method is how we select members from the population to be included in the study.

Three factors influence representativeness: the sampling procedure, the sample size, and participation (response). You might sample the entire population when it is very small, when you have extensive resources, or when you do not expect a high response rate.

2. Population, sample, frame and process

The population is the entire collection of interest; the sample is the subset actually observed. The sampling frame is the practical list from which respondents are drawn — the bridge between the abstract population and the data collected.

Definition — sampling frame

The sampling frame is the list of items or events from which potential respondents are drawn, or which are possible to measure. It must be representative of the population.

Most real sampling failures are frame failures: the frame silently excludes part of the population, and no amount of randomness inside the frame can repair that. The population you sample from may not be exactly the population you want to learn about.

The sampling process:

  1. Define the target population.
  2. Specify the sampling frame.
  3. Specify the sampling method.
  4. Determine the sample size.
  5. Implement the sampling plan.
  6. Collect the data.
  7. Review the sampling process.

EPS design. When every element has the same probability of selection, the design is called equal probability of selection (EPS) or self-weighting. SRS is always EPS, but not every EPS design is SRS — systematic sampling is the counterexample.

4. Simple random sampling (SRS)

Definition (Navidi, p. 3)

A simple random sample of size n is a sample chosen by a method in which each collection of n population items is equally likely to make up the sample, just as in a lottery.

Procedure. Number the N population items 1…N; generate n random integers between 1 and N; select the corresponding items. For a small population, a manual lottery works; for a large one, use a computer generator.

Probability of selection
P(selected) = n / N  ×  100%
N = population size, n = sample size. Example: 5 coins chosen from 20 → 5/20 = 25%. 25 employees from 10,000 → 0.25%.

Advantages: simple to use; estimates easy to calculate; usually fairly representative; sampling error depends on sample size and population variability; requires only minimal prior knowledge of the population.

Disadvantages: a large frame may be costly to compile; minority subgroups may be too rare to study; stratified sampling may be more efficient for heterogeneous populations; a complete catalogue is sometimes unavailable; it ignores available knowledge about the population.

With replacement vs without replacement

5. Systematic sampling

Arrange the population in some order, choose the first element at random from the first k elements, then take every k-th element.

Selection interval
k = N / n
N = population size, n = sample size. A “skip of 10” means every 10th name.

Example (classroom). 64 students, sample 8 → k = 8. Pick a random start among the first 8, then every 8th student. Example (audit). 10,000 purchase orders, sample 50 → k = 200. If the random start is order 45, the sample is 45, 245, 445, 645, …

Why systematic is EPS but not SRS

Every individual has the same chance (4/12 = 1/3 in the illustration), but different subsets have different probabilities. The set {2, 5, 8, 11} has probability 1/3; the set {1, 3, 6, 7} has probability 0. This is why Navidi defines SRS in terms of collections of n items, not individual items.

Advantages: easy to select; frame easy to identify; spreads evenly over the population; cost-effective; guarantees even coverage — but a random start is essential and periodic patterns require care.

Disadvantages: bias when an underlying pattern coincides with the interval (e.g. HR data grouped by team and ordered by seniority could skip junior staff); hard to assess precision from one survey; not every subset is equally likely; elements between the k-th selections are ignored; population size N must be known.

6. Stratified sampling

Divide the population into non-overlapping groups called strata based on a shared characteristic (gender, age, income, grade), then draw a simple random sample from each stratum and combine them. All strata are represented.

“In stratified random sampling, the population is divided up into subpopulations, called strata, and a simple random sample is drawn from each stratum.”

Navidi, p. 10

Properties. Each stratum is sampled as an independent sub-population; every unit within a stratum has the same chance; using the same sampling fraction across strata gives proportionate representation; minority subgroups can be ensured adequate representation by varying the fraction; different sampling approaches may be used in different strata.

Example (radio listeners). Listeners are stratified by age (20–29, 30–39, 40–49). Each stratum is internally homogeneous and the strata are heterogeneous relative to one another; an SRS is taken from each.

Advantages: more representative; easy to carry out; higher statistical efficiency; greater precision than SRS of the same size; often needs a smaller sample, saving money.

Disadvantages: a frame must be prepared for each stratum; multiple stratifying variables can complicate the design; many strata or minimum-per-group rules can require a larger sample; time-consuming and expensive; classification errors.

7. Cluster sampling

Divide the population into non-overlapping clusters, ideally each a miniature of the population. Randomly select whole clusters, then either include every element in the chosen clusters (one-stage) or randomly sample within them (two-stage).

“In cluster sampling, items are drawn from the population in groups, or clusters. Cluster sampling is useful when the population is too large and spread out for simple random sampling to be feasible.”

Navidi, p. 10

Example (Grade 11 sports). Each school is a cluster; 100 schools are randomly selected across Canada, and every Grade 11 student in those schools is surveyed. Example (health care). City blocks are clusters of households; the council randomly picks blocks.

Advantages: convenient for geographically dispersed populations; cuts travel costs; simplifies administration; more feasible; can include more subjects because each cluster represents the population; requires fewer resources.

Disadvantages: statistically less efficient when cluster elements are similar; greater cost and more problems than SRS; higher sampling error; prone to bias if clusters were formed under a biased opinion; hard to guarantee the chosen clusters represent the whole population.

9. Non-probability methods

9.1 Convenience sampling

Also called grab, opportunity, accidental or haphazard sampling. The sample is drawn from the part of the population close at hand; elements are chosen for the researcher's convenience. The textbook definition: “a sample of convenience is a sample that is obtained in some convenient way, and not drawn by a well-defined random method.”

Concrete blocks. Drawing an SRS of 10 blocks from a 1000-block pile would require removing blocks from the centre and bottom; the engineer might instead take 10 off the top. That is a convenience sample. It is acceptable only if he is confident the top blocks do not differ systematically (different batch, curing time or temperature would break this).

Navidi's rule: convenience samples should not be used except where a random sample is infeasible. When one must be taken, think carefully about every way it might differ systematically; if no important difference is plausible, it may be treated as if it were an SRS.

Advantages: useful in pilot studies; inexpensive; saves time; easy to obtain and implement. Disadvantages: significant bias; not generalisable; sampling errors possible; limited external validity.

9.2 Judgemental (purposive) sampling

The researcher chooses the sample based on who they think is appropriate. Used when only a limited number of people have expertise in the area; the sample depends on the judgement of experts; it is not a scientific method of sampling.

Examples: disabled students selected purposefully to capture varied support needs; researchers who understand ethical hacking decide which participants are eligible.

Advantages: minimum time; allows expertise and judgement; no special statistical knowledge; real-time results. Disadvantages: judgement errors; low reliability, high bias; cannot generalise; hard to choose the sample size.

9.3 Quota sampling

The population is segmented into mutually exclusive subgroups (as in stratified sampling), then subjects are selected non-randomly until a specified quota in each segment is filled. It looks like stratified sampling but lacks random selection.

Example: recruit the first 50 men and first 50 women who meet the inclusion criteria; or 500 respondents split 250 male / 250 female, 250 aged 18–50 / 250 aged 51+, 50 per state.

Advantages: cost-effective; convenient; fast; improves representation of certain groups and prevents over-representation. Disadvantages: sampling error cannot be determined; bias if selection is based on ease of access or cost; statistical inference and generalisation are not possible.

The standard trap

Quota ≠ stratified. Same subgroups and proportions, but selection within each subgroup is non-random — which forfeits any estimate of sampling error. Stratified is a probability method; quota is not.

9.4 Snowball sampling

Existing subjects nominate further subjects known to them, so the sample grows like a rolling snowball. Effective when a sampling frame is difficult to identify, or when subjects are hard to trace (shelterless people, illegal immigrants, elite club members).

Chain example: person 1 recommends person 6, who recommends 11, then 14, then 19.

Advantages: reaches populations other methods cannot; cheap, simple, cost-efficient; little planning and few staff. Disadvantages: significant selection bias (referred people share traits with referrers); sampling error cannot be determined; little control; representativeness not guaranteed.

10. Sampling variation and independence

Sampling variation means samples differ from their populations and from each other. Independence is a separate, stronger condition:

Definition — independent items (Navidi, p. 8)

Items in a sample are independent if knowing the values of some of them does not help predict the values of the others.

The finite-population difficulty. In a small population {0, 0, 1, 1}, the second draw is clearly influenced by the first, so the items are dependent. In a population of one million 0s and one million 1s, the second draw is almost unaffected, so the items are practically independent.

The 5% rule of thumb
treat items as independent if   n ≤ 0.05 N
When sampling without replacement from a finite population, independence is a safe approximation as long as the sample is at most 5% of the population.

Other routes to independence. Sampling with replacement keeps the population identical on every draw. A conceptual population gives independence when every observation is produced under identical experimental conditions, so no value influences another. Population size alone is not enough — the measurement process must also satisfy the condition.

11. Sample size and the statistic/parameter distinction

The more heterogeneous the population, the larger the sample needed. For probability sampling, larger is better; for non-probability samples, size does not make the result generalisable. The main factors are the total population size, the margin of error, and the confidence level — the last two are quantified in Sessions 36–39.

12. Errors in sampling

Sampling (random) errorNon-sampling error
Arises from random variation between a sample and its populationArises from coverage, response, measurement or data-processing mistakes
Can be measured and reduced (not eliminated) under random samplingCan be random or systematic; not fixed by a larger sample

Sampling error is the discrepancy between a sample statistic and its population parameter. For a given sample it is unknown, but under random sampling theory can measure the variation for estimators such as X̄ or p̂. It arises because only part of the population is observed.

Non-sampling errors include: failure to locate the correct household; misunderstanding of questions; data-entry errors; missing data; poorly conceived concepts or defective questionnaires; response errors (people unaware, refuse, or overstate). Major sources are sampling bias and non-response bias.

Sampling bias is a systematic tendency for selection to misrepresent the population. It arises from the sampling technique or data-collection method and can be selection bias or non-response bias. Unequal inclusion probabilities do not by themselves imply bias: known probabilities can be corrected with sampling weights.

Selection bias is a systematic distortion caused by the way participants are selected. Non-response bias occurs when respondents differ systematically from non-respondents — through refusal, failure to contact, or lack of interest.

13. Worked examples on method identification

Example 1.1 — the professor's random-number sample

Simple random sampling. The 100 students correspond to 100 random integers; equivalent to a lottery.

Example 1.2 — the wallpaper inspector (why it is not SRS)

Each hour for 5 hours she takes the 10 most recently produced rolls. No, this is not an SRS. Not every subset of 50 rolls is equally likely: rolls produced outside those collection times have no chance of selection.

The sample is spread across the day, yet still fails — proof that randomness is a property of the method, not of the data's appearance.

Concrete blocks — which method is suitable?

Taking 10 blocks off the top of the pile is a convenience sample, because an SRS would require reaching the centre and bottom.

LED bulbs — every 10th vs by shift

(i) Every 10th bulb off the line = systematic sampling. (ii) Divide production into morning/evening/night shifts and sample from each = stratified sampling.

15. Key takeaways

  • Randomness is a property of the method, not of the resulting data. The wallpaper sample is spread through the day and is still not an SRS.
  • The great divide is probability vs non-probability. Only probability methods allow sampling error to be estimated — and that is what the rest of Unit 3 does.
  • Navidi's definition concerns collections of n items, not individual items. Systematic sampling proves the distinction is real.
  • Strata are internally homogeneous and all sampled; clusters are internally heterogeneous and only some chosen.
  • Quota is not stratified: same subgroups and proportions, but non-random selection within them.
  • Convenience is a last resort; ask in what systematic way it might differ from the population.
  • Randomness buys analyzability, not accuracy. The 5% rule licenses independence for finite populations; replacement and conceptual populations give true independence.
  • No increase in sample size fixes a biased frame or a defective questionnaire.
Unit 3 · Session 31Probability models·

Random variables, binomial & normal distributions

How to attach numbers to outcomes, describe them completely with a pmf, and work every standard binomial and normal problem.

In one line: a random variable turns outcomes into numbers; its distribution lists those numbers with probabilities; the binomial counts successes in independent trials, and the normal describes continuous variation through z-scores.

1. What a random variable is

An electrical engineer has six resistors: three labelled 10 Ω (actual 9, 10, 11 Ω) and three labelled 20 Ω (actual 19, 20, 21 Ω). Selecting one of each gives nine equally likely outcomes. What matters is the sum of the two resistances, so assign to each outcome its sum. Call that assignment X.

Definition

A random variable assigns a numerical value to each outcome in a sample space. Random variables are usually denoted by uppercase letters X, Y, Z.

3. Probability distributions

Probability mass function (pmf)
p(x) = P(X = x)
For a discrete X. Also called the probability distribution. Sums to 1 over all possible values.
Cumulative distribution function (cdf)
F(x) = P(X ≤ x) = Σt ≤ x p(t)
Accumulates all probability up to and including x.

Wire-flaw illustration. 48% of wires have no flaws, 39% one, 12% two, 1% three. So p(0)=0.48, p(1)=0.39, p(2)=0.12, p(3)=0.01, and p(x)=0 otherwise. The sum is 1 — a complete description of the population from which X is drawn.

Random variables and populations. Observing X is like sampling one value from a population of values in the given proportions. For a continuous variable, imagine an infinite population containing every possible value; the proportion in any interval equals the probability the variable falls there.

4. The binomial distribution

A single component is defective or not: a Bernoulli trial. Sample several independent components from a large lot and count defectives: the count is a binomial random variable.

Definition — binomial

If n Bernoulli trials are conducted, the trials are independent, each has the same success probability p, and X is the number of successes, then

X ~ Bin(n, p), with possible values 0, 1, …, n.

Check all three conditions

Independent trials · constant p · X counts successes. The 5% rule is what licenses independence when sampling a finite lot: if n ≤ 0.05N, the binomial may be used.

Example 4.5. A fair coin is tossed 10 times; X = number of heads → X ~ Bin(10, 0.5). Example 4.6. A lot has 10% defectives; 7 components are sampled (small relative to the lot) → X ~ Bin(7, 0.1).

4.1 Deriving the pmf

A biased coin has P(heads) = 0.6 and is tossed three times. For two heads, the arrangements are HHT, HTH, THH. Each has probability (0.6)²(0.4)¹, so

Binomial probability mass function
p(x) = (n! / [x!(n−x)!]) · px(1−p)n−x,   x = 0,1,…,n
p(x) = 0 otherwise. n! = n(n−1)···2·1 and 0! = 1. The combinatorial factor counts arrangements.

4.2 Mean and variance

Binomial mean
μX = n p
Binomial variance
σ²X = n p (1 − p)

Why. Write X = Y₁ + ⋯ + Yn, where Yi = 1 on success and 0 on failure. Each Yi has mean p and variance p(1−p); means and variances of independent variables add. This identity is the bridge to the CLT in Session 32.

p(5) = 10!/[5!5!] (0.4)⁵(0.6)⁵ = 0.2007.

X ~ Bin(8, 1/6). P(X ≤ 2) = P(0)+P(1)+P(2) = 0.2326 + 0.3721 + 0.2605 = 0.8652.

Examples 4.9 & 4.10 — table technique and the complement

X ~ Bin(12, 0.1). “Fewer than 4” = P(X ≤ 3) = 0.974 from Table A.1.

“More than 1” = 1 − P(X ≤ 1) = 1 − 0.659 = 0.341. Always express “more than” via the complement because the table gives P(X ≤ x).

5. The normal distribution

The normal (Gaussian) distribution is the most commonly used continuous model. Part of the reason is the Central Limit Theorem (next session). Its mean may be any value and its variance any positive value.

Normal probability density function
f(x) = 1 / (σ√(2π)) · e−(x − μ)² / (2σ²)
If X has this density, write X ~ N(μ, σ²). Then μX = μ and σ²X = σ².

Properties of the normal curve. It is symmetric about μ, so μ is both the mean and the median. For any normal population:

Within 2 sd

Within 3 sd

5.1 Standard units and z-scores

z-score (standard units)
z = (x − μ) / σ
z tells how many standard deviations x lies from the mean. z itself is a draw from the standard normal population, N(0, 1).

Example 4.38. Women's heights: μ = 64 in, σ = 3 in. 67 in → z = (67−64)/3 = 1.00. 62 in → z = (62−64)/3 = −0.67.

Example 4.39. Aluminium thickness N(10, 1.3²); x = 10.8 → z = 0.62. Example 4.40 (backwards). z = −1.7 → x = 10 + (−1.7)(1.3) = 7.8 thousandths of an inch.

The Central Limit Theorem and its applications

Why sample means are approximately normal even when the population is not — and how this makes the z-table universal.

In one line: for a sufficiently large independent sample from a population with finite mean and variance, the sample mean is approximately normal — so we can use z even if the population is skewed, discrete or unknown.

1. The population of sample means

Let X₁, …, Xn be a simple random sample from a population with mean μ and variance σ². The sample mean is

Sample mean
X̄ = (X₁ + ⋯ + Xn) / n

Imagine drawing every possible sample of size n and computing each sample mean. That collection is the population of sample means. The surprising fact is that, for large n, its shape does not depend on the shape of the original population.

2. Statement of the theorem

Central Limit Theorem (Navidi, Section 4.11, p. 290)

Let X₁, …, Xn be an independent random sample with finite mean μ and variance σ² > 0. Let X̄ be the sample mean and Sn = X₁ + ⋯ + Xn the sample sum. Then if n is sufficiently large,

X̄ ~ N(μ, σ²/n) approximately, and Sn ~ N(nμ, nσ²) approximately.

Sample mean
X̄ ~ N(μ, σ²/n)
Mean μ; variance σ²/n; standard deviation σ/√n.
Sample sum
Sn ~ N(nμ, nσ²)
Mean nμ; variance nσ²; standard deviation σ√n.

What the theorem does and does not say. The mean μ and variance σ²/n of X̄ hold for any sample mean, normal or not. The content of the theorem is the shape: approximate normality. Since Sn = nX̄, its mean is nμ and its variance is n²(σ²/n) = nσ².

3. How large is large enough?

It depends on the shape of the population. A nearly symmetric population needs only a small n; a heavily skewed population needs a larger n. Empirical evidence suggests that for most populations n ≥ 30 is adequate.

  • The CLT supplies the shape of X̄; the mean μ and variance σ²/n were true all along.

Point estimation, bias, variance and MSE

How to judge an estimator: accuracy is bias, precision is standard error, and MSE combines both.

In one line: an estimator is good if it is close on average (unbiased) and consistent from sample to sample (low variance); MSE = bias² + variance scores both at once.

1. Estimator vs estimate

TermMeaningExample
StatisticA quantity calculated from dataX̄, s², p̂
ParameterAn unknown constant to be estimated, denoted θμ, σ², p
Point estimateA statistic used to estimate θ, when a particular numerical value is specifiedp̂ = 3/10 when X = 3

Often “estimator” and “estimate” are used loosely, but Navidi is careful: estimate = specific number, estimator = random variable. The whole section answers two questions: (1) how good is a given estimator? (2) how do we construct a good one? This session answers (1); Session 34 answers (2).

2. Accuracy and precision

Bias — accuracy
bias = μθ̂ − θ
The difference between the mean of the estimator and the true value. Zero bias = unbiased.
Uncertainty — precision

How close repeated values of the estimator are to each other.

MSEθ̂ = (μθ̂ − θ)² + σ²θ̂
Square of the bias plus the variance of the estimator.

MSEθ̂ = E[(θ̂ − θ)²]
The mean of the squared error — which is exactly where the name comes from.

4. Worked examples

Example 4.67 — MSE of the sample proportion

For X ~ Bin(n, p), p̂ = X/n is unbiased and has variance p(1−p)/n. Therefore

MSE(p̂) = 0 + p(1−p)/n = p(1−p)/n.

Example 4.68 — trading a little bias for a lot of variance (normal population)

Let X₁, …, Xn ~ N(μ, σ²). The usual sample variance is s² = Σ(Xi − X̄)²/(n−1), with E[s²] = σ² and Var(s²) = 2σ⁴/(n−1). Consider the MLE-style estimator σ̂² = Σ(Xi − X̄)²/n = [(n−1)/n]s².

s²: unbiased → MSE(s²) = 2σ⁴/(n−1).

σ̂² bias: E[σ̂²] = [(n−1)/n]σ², so bias = −σ²/n.

σ̂² variance: [(n−1)²/n²]·[2σ⁴/(n−1)] = 2(n−1)σ⁴/n².

σ̂² MSE: (−σ²/n)² + 2(n−1)σ⁴/n² = [(2n−1)/n²]σ⁴.

Compare: MSE(s²) − MSE(σ̂²) = σ⁴(3n−1)/[n²(n−1)] > 0 for n > 1. So σ̂² has the smaller MSE despite being biased.

Lesson: unbiased is not automatically best. MSE is the proper overall criterion. This biased estimator reappears as the MLE in the next session.

5. Key takeaways

  • Estimation is the point of collecting data. A statistic becomes an estimator when used for an unknown parameter.
  • Say estimate for a specific number, estimator for the random quantity.
  • Judge on two counts: accuracy (bias) and precision (standard deviation). Neither alone is enough.
  • MSE = bias² + variance; equivalently the mean of the squared error.
  • For an unbiased estimator, MSE reduces to the variance.
  • Unbiased is not the same as best: Example 4.68 shows the biased σ̂² can have lower MSE for every n > 1.

Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE)

Constructing estimators by choosing the parameter value that makes the observed data most likely — with full derivations for Bernoulli, binomial, Poisson, normal and exponential models.

In one line: hold the data fixed, move the distribution, and pick the parameter value under which the data you actually saw is most probable.

1. The idea

Session 33 judged estimators. This session constructs them. The method of maximum likelihood estimates a parameter with the value that makes the observed data most likely. Equivalently: assume a probability model with unknown parameters and choose the parameter values that maximise the likelihood of the observed sample.

Definitions (Navidi, p. 282)

When a probability mass or density function is considered as a function of parameters, it is called a likelihood function. The maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) is the value which, when substituted for the parameter, maximises the likelihood function.

1.1 Likelihood is not probability

ProbabilityLikelihood
What it isAreas under a fixed distributiony-axis values for fixed data, with the distribution allowed to move
WrittenP(data | distribution)L(distribution | data)
What variesThe dataThe distribution (parameter)
What is fixedThe distributionThe data

Navidi's notation f(7; p) — rather than f(7) — makes the point: the semicolon separates the fixed data value 7 from the parameter p being varied.

2. The four-step procedure

  1. Write the likelihood function (the joint pmf/pdf of the sample, as a function of the parameter).
  2. Take the natural logarithm. The maximiser of ln L is the maximiser of L, and logs turn products into sums.
  3. Differentiate the log-likelihood with respect to the parameter.
  4. Solve for stationary points, check boundaries, and verify it is a maximum.
Why take logs?

Differentiating a product of n factors needs the product rule repeatedly. The log converts ∏ f(xi) into Σ ln f(xi), which is far easier. This is legitimate because the maximiser of ln L is the maximiser of L.

Log identities used: log(ab) = log a + log b; log(ab) = b log a; ln e = 1.

3. General definition

Let X₁, …, Xn have joint density or mass function f(x₁, …, xn; θ₁, …, θk). The values θ₁, …, θk that maximise f are the maximum likelihood estimates. If the random variables are substituted for the observed values, they are maximum likelihood estimators. MLE abbreviates both.

4. MLE for the Bernoulli distribution

Let X₁, …, Xn ~ Bernoulli(p). Each pmf is f(xi; p) = pxi(1−p)1−xi, xi ∈ {0,1}.

Step 1 — likelihood
L(p) = ∏ pxi(1−p)1−xi = pΣxi(1−p)n−Σxi
Step 2 — log-likelihood
ln L = (Σxi) ln p + (n − Σxi) ln(1−p)
Steps 3–4 — differentiate and solve
d(ln L)/dp = (Σxi)/p − (n − Σxi)/(1−p) = 0
Clearing denominators gives Σxi(1−p) − (n−Σxi)p = 0, hence Σxi − np = 0.
Bernoulli MLE
p̂ML = Σxi / n = x̄
The sample proportion — exactly what intuition expects.

5. MLE for the binomial distribution

Navidi develops the method through a worked instance. Let X ~ Bin(20, p) and observe X = 7. Then

Likelihood
f(7; p) = [20!/(7!13!)] p⁷(1−p)¹³

Log-likelihood
ln f(7; p) = ln 20! − ln 7! − ln 13! + 7 ln p + 13 ln(1−p)
Maximise

For any observed X, the maximiser is X/20, so the estimator is

Estimate when a specific X is known; estimator when X is random.

9. Why MLEs dominate practice

In most practical cases MLEs have two desirable properties:

  1. As n increases, the bias converges to 0.
  2. As n increases, the variance converges to a theoretical minimum.

Together these say the MSE (bias² + variance) approaches the best attainable value as n grows — the real justification for the method's dominance.

10. Pitfalls of point estimators

  • A point estimate is a single number that varies from sample to sample.
  • It may differ from the true parameter by a little or a lot.
  • It cannot be confidently claimed to be close to the parameter.
  • The fix is an interval centred on the point estimate — a confidence interval (Sessions 36–39).

11. Key takeaways

  • MSE judges an estimator; maximum likelihood constructs one.
  • The principle: choose the parameter value that makes the observed data most likely.
  • Likelihood fixes the data and moves the distribution; probability fixes the distribution and varies the data.
  • Write the likelihood, take logs, differentiate, solve, and check the maximum and boundaries.
  • For an independent sample, the likelihood is the product of marginals — this is what makes every derivation tractable.
  • Normal: μ̂ = X̄ and σ̂² = Σ(Xi−X̄)²/n. The divisor is n, so σ̂² is biased but has lower MSE than s².
  • MLEs are preferred because bias → 0 and variance → the theoretical minimum as n grows.
  • A point estimate is still one number; the confidence interval is the remedy.

Multivariate normal distribution & MAP estimation

From one variable to a vector of correlated variables, and from maximum likelihood to maximum a posteriori estimation.

In one line: the multivariate normal generalises the bell curve to correlated vectors; MAP adds a prior to likelihood and estimates the posterior mode.

1. From univariate to multivariate statistics

Earlier sessions dealt with one variable at a time (univariate statistics). Multivariate statistics analyses several variables together, allowing us to understand relationships — for example, how house prices relate to year, size and location. Just as the normal distribution is central in univariate statistics, the multivariate normal (MVN) is central in multivariate statistics.

Definition — MVN

The multivariate normal distribution describes the joint distribution of a vector of random variables, each normally distributed and possibly correlated with the others. A random vector X = (X₁, X₂, …, Xn)T follows an MVN with mean vector μ ∈ ℝn and covariance matrix Σ ∈ ℝn×n, written

X ~ Nn(μ, Σ).

2. Covariance and the covariance matrix

Covariance (bivariate)
Cov(X, Y) = E[(X − μX)(Y − μY)]
Quantifies how two variables move together. Positive: Y tends to rise with X. Negative: Y tends to fall as X rises. Zero: no linear co-movement.

For a sample, the usual estimator is sXY = Σ(xi − x̄)(yi − ȳ)/(n−1). The covariance matrix Σ has variables as rows and columns; the diagonal holds variances and the off-diagonals hold covariances. It is the multivariate counterpart of variance, and it describes the spread and orientation of the MVN cloud.

Reading a covariance matrix

Diagonal entries are variances σ² of individual variables. Off-diagonal entry (i, j) is Cov(Xi, Xj) = Cov(Xj, Xi), so the matrix is symmetric.

Portfolio example (slides). Three asset classes — stocks, bonds, real estate — with return vector μ and covariance matrix

The diagonal gives variances 0.09 (stocks), 0.01 (bonds), 0.07 (real estate). Off-diagonals give covariances: stocks–bonds 0.020, stocks–real estate 0.040, bonds–real estate 0.010. Portfolio optimisation uses these to trade expected return against risk.

3. The MVN density

Multivariate normal pdf
f(x) = (2π)−n/2 |Σ|−1/2 exp[ −½ (x − μ)T Σ−1 (x − μ) ]
|Σ| is the determinant of the covariance matrix; Σ−1 its inverse. The quadratic form in the exponent is the squared Mahalanobis distance from x to μ.

Bivariate case. With two variables, equal variances σ² and correlation ρ, the exponent becomes

Bivariate normal exponent
− 1/[2(1−ρ²)] · [ ((x₁−μ₁)/σ)² − 2ρ((x₁−μ₁)/σ)((x₂−μ₂)/σ) + ((x₂−μ₂)/σ)² ]
ρ = 0 makes the density factor into two independent normals — uncorrelated jointly normal variables are independent.

Key properties. Every marginal of an MVN is normal; every linear combination of an MVN vector is normal; the distribution is fully specified by μ and Σ; and uncorrelated jointly normal variables are independent (a property that is not true in general).

5. Maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation

MAP finds the mode (peak) of the posterior distribution of a parameter. In Bayesian inference the posterior is our updated belief after observing data; the MAP estimate is the parameter value that maximises that posterior probability.

Bayes' theorem for a parameter
g(θ | x) = f(x | θ) g(θ) / f(x)
g(θ|x) = posterior; f(x|θ) = likelihood; g(θ) = prior; f(x) = marginal likelihood (a normalising constant).
MAP estimate
θ̂MAP = argmaxθ g(θ | x) = argmaxθ [ f(x | θ) g(θ) ]
The denominator f(x) is positive and does not depend on θ, so it plays no role in the optimisation. Equivalently maximise ln f(x|θ) + ln g(θ).
Relationship to MLE

MAP is MLE with a prior. If the prior g(θ) is uniform (constant), the prior term drops out and MAP coincides with MLE. MAP can therefore be seen as a regularisation of maximum likelihood: the prior pulls the estimate toward plausible values.

Worked pattern (normal mean with normal prior). If the likelihood is N(x̄ | θ, σ²/n) and the prior is N(θ | μ₀, τ²), completing the square gives a posterior that is also normal, with precision-weighted mean

Normal–normal MAP
θ̂MAP = [ (n/σ²)x̄ + (1/τ²)μ₀ ] / [ n/σ² + 1/τ² ]
A weighted average of the data mean and the prior mean, weighted by their precisions. Strong prior (small τ²) pulls the estimate toward μ₀.

7. Key takeaways

  • The MVN generalises the normal to vectors: X ~ Nn(μ, Σ), specified entirely by a mean vector and covariance matrix.
  • Covariance measures joint movement; the covariance matrix has variances on the diagonal and covariances off it.
  • The MVN density uses the inverse and determinant of Σ; the exponent is a squared Mahalanobis distance.
  • Marginals and linear combinations of an MVN are normal; zero covariance between jointly normal variables implies independence.
  • MAP estimates the posterior mode: maximise likelihood × prior.
  • With a uniform prior, MAP = MLE; more generally MAP is a regularised MLE.

Unit 3 · Session 36Confidence intervals·

Confidence intervals: the idea

Why a single number is never a complete answer, what "95% confident" actually means, and the one distinction examiners love to test.

In one line: a point estimate says where the parameter probably is; a confidence interval says how far off the estimate is likely to be.

1. Why a point estimate is not enough

In Chapter 4 (Sessions 33–34) we produced point estimates: the sample mean x̄ for a population mean μ, and the sample proportion p̂ for a success probability p. These are single numbers — points.

The core problem

A point estimate is almost never exactly equal to the true value. It is almost always off — sometimes a little, sometimes a lot. To be useful, we must say how far off it is likely to be.

One way to do that is to report the standard deviation (standard error) of the estimate. When the estimate comes from a normal distribution, we can do better: we can compute a confidence interval, an interval that is very likely to contain the parameter.

This answers the quality engineer's second question from Session 29: instead of "92%", report "92% ± x%", with x chosen so that we have reasonable certainty.

2. The piston example — where the normal curve comes in

A large number of independent, unbiased measurements are made of a piston's diameter. The sample mean is x̄ = 14.0 cm and the standard deviation of the sample mean (the standard error) is σx̄ = 0.1 cm.

The value 14.0 comes from a normal distribution because it is the average of many measurements — that is the Central Limit Theorem (Session 32). So we can reason with the 68–95–99.7 rule:

IntervalWidth in standard errorsConfidence that μ is insideWhy
(13.9, 14.1)14.0 ± 1(0.1)68%An x̄ more than 1 SE from μ is not unusual.
(13.7, 14.3)14.0 ± 3(0.1)99.7%An x̄ more than 3 SE from μ is very unlikely.

Notice the direction of the reasoning. Session 31 asked: given μ, where does x̄ fall? A confidence interval asks the reverse: given x̄, where does μ fall? The arithmetic is the same; the interpretation is inverted.

3. What a confidence level means

Definition — level 100(1 − α)% confidence interval

An interval computed by a method that, in the long run, succeeds in covering the population mean a proportion 1 − α of all the times it is used.

The confidence level measures the reliability of the method, not the probability that any one computed interval contains μ. This is the single most important sentence in the chapter, and Section 5 below expands it.

4. Reliability versus precision — the trade-off

Intervals with greater confidence are wider, and therefore less precise. For the same data:

68% interval
x̄ ± 1.00 σx̄
Narrow, precise — but fails to cover μ about 32% of the time.
95% interval
x̄ ± 1.96 σx̄
About half the precision of the 68% interval, but far more reliable.
99.7% interval
x̄ ± 3.00 σx̄
Only 3 failures in 1000 — but the widest and least informative.

Navidi's Figure 5.4 makes the point visually: 100 samples from the same population give 100 different intervals, because each has a different x̄. At 68% roughly 32 of them miss μ; at 95% about 5 miss; at 99.7% roughly none miss.

What is used in practice
  • 95% is the most common choice — a good compromise between reliability and precision.
  • Levels below 90% are rarely used; a 68% interval fails too often to be useful.
  • 99.7% (or 99%) is reserved for quality-assurance work where product reliability matters more than precision.

Two limiting cases fix the idea permanently: we are 100% confident that μ lies in (−∞, ∞), and 0% confident that it lies in the zero-width interval [x̄, x̄]. Confidence is bought with width.

5. Probability versus confidence

Suppose a 95% interval for mean fill weight is (12.304, 12.696). It is tempting to say "the probability is 95% that μ is in this interval". That is wrong.

The distinction

12.304, 12.696 and μ are all fixed numbers. μ is either inside the interval or it is not; there is no randomness left. Probability applies to random events that can differ when an experiment is repeated. So we say we have 95% confidence, not 95% probability.

But a method for computing 95% intervals does cover μ 95% of the time. Coverage is a random event, because it changes from sample to sample. So it is correct to say the method has probability 0.95 of covering μ.

Statement about…Correct wordReason
A specific interval already computedConfidenceEndpoints and μ are fixed; nothing is random.

6. Worked examples

Example 5.5 · A computed interval

A 90% confidence interval for the mean diameter of steel rods is (14.73, 14.91). True or false: the probability that the mean diameter is between 14.73 and 14.91 is 90%.

Show solution

False. This is a specific interval that has already been computed. The mean is either in it or not. We are 90% confident that μ lies between 14.73 and 14.91; "probability" is inappropriate.

Example 5.6 · A method about to be used

An engineer plans to compute a 90% interval x̄ ± 1.645 s/√n from a large sample. True or false: the probability that μ will be in this interval is 90%.

Show solution

True. This describes a method, not a computed interval. A method for a 90% interval has probability 0.90 of covering μ.

Examples 5.5 and 5.6 are deliberately paired: the only difference is tense — has been computed versus will be computed.

Example 5.7 · 250 intervals

Geologists will measure the weights of 250 rocks and compute a 95% interval for each. Assuming no bias and independence, what is the probability that more than 240 of the intervals cover the true weights?

Show solution

Each interval is a Bernoulli trial with success probability p = 0.95. Let Y be the number that cover. Then

Y ~ Bin(250, 0.95) ≈ N(237.5, 11.875)

Mean = 250(0.95) = 237.5; variance = 250(0.95)(0.05) = 11.875; σ = √11.875 = 3.446.

With continuity correction, P(Y > 240) = P(Y ≥ 240.5):

z = (240.5 − 237.5)/3.446 = 0.87 ⇒ P = 1 − 0.8078 = 0.1922

So the probability is about 0.1922. This example is the payoff of the distinction: because the method has probability 0.95 of covering, 250 intervals form a genuine binomial experiment.

7. Key takeaways

  • A point estimate without an uncertainty statement is incomplete.
  • A confidence interval reuses the normal curve in reverse: from x̄ back to μ.
  • The confidence level describes the method's long-run success rate, not a probability about one computed interval.
  • Reliability and precision trade off at fixed n. Larger n is the only way to improve one without sacrificing the other.
  • 95% is the standard practical choice; below 90% is rarely used.

Unit 3 · Session 37z-intervals·

Large-sample confidence interval for μ

The formula x̄ ± zα/2·σ/√n — where it comes from, every standard multiplier, sample-size planning, and one-sided bounds.

In one line: when n > 30, x̄ is approximately normal, so put the middle 1 − α of the normal curve around x̄.

1. Building the formula — the cereal-box example

A quality engineer draws a simple random sample of n = 100 cereal boxes from one day's production. He finds x̄ = 12.05 oz and s = 0.1 oz. He wants an interval around 12.05 that is likely to contain the unknown μ.

By the CLT, x̄ is approximately normal with mean μ and standard deviation

Standard error of the mean
σx̄ = σ / √n
Since σ is unknown, replace it with s when n is large: σx̄ ≈ s/√n = 0.1/√100 = 0.01.

The middle 95% of the normal curve lies within 1.96 standard deviations of the centre. An interval of exactly that length, drawn around x̄, covers μ whenever x̄ happened to fall in the middle 95% — which happens for 95% of all samples.

Cereal box, 95% interval
12.05 ± 1.96(0.01) = 12.05 ± 0.0196 = (12.0304, 12.0696)
We are 95% confident that the population mean fill weight is between 12.0304 and 12.0696 oz.

Figure 5.1 of the text shows the favourable case — x̄ inside the middle 95%, so the interval covers μ. Figure 5.2 shows the unfavourable case — x̄ in one of the 5% tails, so the interval misses μ. The interval does not "know" which case it is in; the method is right 95% of the time.

Repeating the same reasoning for the middle 68% gives the 68% interval: 12.05 ± (1.0)(0.01) = (12.04, 12.06). Wider confidence ⇒ wider interval.

2. The general formula

Definition — zα/2

Let 0 < α < 1. The symbol zα/2 is the z-score that cuts off an area α/2 in the right-hand tail of the standard normal curve. By symmetry, −zα/2 cuts off α/2 in the left tail. For example, z0.025 = 1.96.

Large-sample CI for a population mean — Eq. (5.1)
x̄ ± zα/2 · σ/√n
Valid for a large random sample (n > 30) from a population with mean μ and standard deviation σ, so that x̄ is approximately normal. When σ is unknown, replace it with s. The level is 100(1 − α)%.

5. Choosing the sample size for a target width

The half-width (the quantity after ±) is zα/2σ/√n. If the level and σ are known, set this equal to the desired half-width and solve for n.

Sample size for a specified half-width h
n = ( zα/2 · σ / h )²   → always round up
Because n sits under a square root, precision is expensive: halving the half-width requires about four times the data.

Worked instance. The 95% interval 12.68 ± 1.89 is too wide; we want ±0.50. With z0.025 = 1.96 and σ ≈ 6.83:

n = [ (1.96)(6.83)/0.50 ]² = 716.83 → n = 717
Example 5.8 · Sample size for ±0.012 oz at 99%

With s = 0.1 oz from 100 boxes, how many boxes are needed for a 99% interval of width ±0.012 oz?

Show solution

99% ⇒ z0.005 = 2.58.

n = [ (2.58)(0.1)/0.012 ]² = 462.25 → n = 463

Always round up: 462 would give slightly less than 99% confidence at the required width.

Common error

Do not round down and do not report a fractional sample size. Rounding up guarantees at least the requested precision; rounding down silently breaks the promise you made about width.

6. One-sided confidence intervals

Sometimes only one direction matters. A reliability engineer estimating the mean crushing strength of concrete blocks cares about a lower bound — the blocks must be at least strong enough. There is no penalty for being stronger than necessary.

For 95% of samples, x̄ < μ + 1.645σx̄, so the interval (x̄ − 1.645σx̄, ∞) covers μ. It fails only when x̄ is in the upper 5% tail.

7. The data must be a random sample

Equation (5.1) assumes the observations are a random sample from a population. A trend or cycle in time-ordered data breaks independence and invalidates the method, however large n is.

Example 5.10 · A cyclic pattern

Yields from 100 runs of a chemical process, plotted against time, show a repeating cycle. Should Eq. (5.1) be used?

Show solution

No. The cycle suggests each run is influenced by the previous run (violating independence) or by regularly fluctuating ambient conditions. The data are not a random sample, so the interval is not valid.

Example 5.11 · A learning trend

Yields from another 100 runs tend to increase with time, especially early on. Should Eq. (5.1) be used?

Show solution

No. This looks like a learning effect: the operator improves with experience. A more careful analysis might locate the time where improvement stops and use only the later data — but the full series is not a random sample.

This closes the loop with Session 30: plotting against time is exactly how we check whether data can be treated as a simple random sample.

8. Key takeaways

  • The whole derivation is one geometric idea: take an interval as long as the middle 1 − α of the normal curve and centre it on x̄.
  • For large n, s may replace σ — that is what makes the formula usable.
  • The same equation answers four exam questions: build the interval, change the level, recover the level, and plan the sample size.
  • Sample size grows with the square of the required precision.
  • One-sided bounds use zα and are tighter than the corresponding two-sided limit.
  • No random sample, no valid interval.

Unit 3 · Session 38t-intervals·

Small samples & the Student's t distribution

Why z fails when n is small, what degrees of freedom do, how to read the t table, and the normality assumption you must check.

In one line: when n is small, s is a shaky stand-in for σ, so replace the normal curve with a heavier-tailed t curve indexed by n − 1 degrees of freedom.

1. Why the large-sample method fails

If x̄ is the mean of a large sample, the CLT gives x̄ ~ N(μ, σ²/n), so (x̄ − μ)/(σ/√n) is exactly standard normal. For large n, s is almost certainly close to σ, so (x̄ − μ)/(s/√n) is also approximately standard normal and the z table works.

For small n, two things break at once:

  1. s may not be close to σ, so substituting s for σ is no longer harmless.
  2. x̄ may not be approximately normal, because the CLT no longer applies.
The honest position

When the sample is small there are no good general methods for confidence intervals. But when the population is approximately normal, a distribution called the Student's t can be used.

Normality removes problem 2 by assumption. The t distribution handles problem 1, because s is still random but we now account for its extra uncertainty.

2. The t statistic and degrees of freedom

Definition — Student's t statistic

Let X₁, …, Xn be a random sample (n ≥ 2) from a normal population with mean μ. Then

t = (x̄ − μ) / (s/√n)

has a Student's t distribution with n − 1 degrees of freedom, written tn−1.

As n grows, this distribution becomes indistinguishable from the standard normal, and the z table may be used instead.

Degrees of freedom (df) = one less than the sample size. A sample of 10 observations uses t₉. The reason is that s is computed from deviations around x̄, and those n deviations are constrained to sum to zero — so only n − 1 of them carry independent information.

History, in one sentence

William Sealy Gosset discovered the distribution in 1908 while working for Guinness in Dublin. Forbidden by his employer to publish, he used the pseudonym "Student".

3. Shape of the t curves

All t curves are symmetric, bell-shaped and centred at 0, like z — but they are more spread out, with heavier tails.

6. Is t appropriate? Check the assumption

The t method requires an approximately normal population. Sometimes past experience tells you this; often you must judge from the sample. But with small samples, departures from normality are hard to detect.

Practical check

Make a boxplot or dotplot. If there is no strong asymmetry and no outliers, the t distribution is usually reliable. A probability plot also works in principle, but is harder to draw by hand for small samples.

Outliers — a hard stop

Do not use the t statistic if the sample contains outliers. A sample from a normal population rarely contains outliers, so an outlier is evidence the assumption fails. The method is then invalid, not merely approximate.

Unit 3 · Session 39Precision·

What makes an interval wide or narrow

The half-width tn−1,α/2·s/√n has three ingredients and four levers. Knowing which lever is free is the whole art of designing a study.

In one line: confidence level widens the interval, sample size narrows it twice, population variability widens it proportionally, and one-sided bounds buy tightness by spending α on one side only.

Terminology note

"Margin of error" does not appear in Navidi 4e. The textbook says width and precision. The quantity the syllabus calls margin of error is exactly the half-width:

half-width = tn−1, α/2 × s/√n

1. The three ingredients

IngredientWhat it isUnder whose control
tn−1, α/2Multiplier depending on confidence level and dfExperimenter chooses the level; n fixes df

2. Factor 1 — Confidence level

Raising 100(1 − α)% means reducing α, which pushes tn−1,α/2 further into the tail and makes it larger. The interval widens.

LevelWidthCoverage behaviourVerdict
68%Narrowest, most preciseMisses μ 32% of the timeUnacceptable for practical use
95%IntermediateAbout 95% cover μGood compromise — the standard choice
99.7%Widest, least preciseFails only 3 times in 1000Extremely reliable, but uninformative about μ

For large samples the comparison is concrete: 68% specifies μ to within ±1.0σx̄; 95% only to within ±1.96σx̄ — about half the precision. The limiting cases settle it: 100% confidence requires (−∞, ∞), and zero width gives 0% confidence.

Verdict: raising confidence always widens the interval. That is the definition of reliability, not a defect to engineer away.

3. Factor 2 — Sample size (it acts twice)

Through √n in the denominator

s/√n is the estimated standard error. Increasing n shrinks it directly. For a target half-width h:

Sample-size planning (large-sample form)
zα/2 σ/√n = h  ⇒  n = (zα/2σ/h)²

Worked instance. The microdrill 95% interval 12.68 ± 1.89 is too wide. To reach ±0.50, solve (1.96)(6.83)/√n = 0.50 ⇒ n = 716.83 ⇒ 717.

Example 5.8: s = 0.1 oz, 99% level, target ±0.012 oz ⇒ (2.58)(0.1)/√n = 0.012 ⇒ n = 462.25 ⇒ 463.

Why precision is expensive

Because the denominator is √n, halving the width costs roughly four times the data. Going from ±1.89 to ±0.50 is a factor of ~4 in width but a factor of ~14 in sample size (50 → 717).

Through the degrees of freedom

This effect is peculiar to the t interval, and works the same way. A small sample means s is often far below σ, producing large |t| values — so the curve has heavy tails and a large critical value. As df grows, s becomes trustworthy, the curve narrows toward z, and the multiplier falls.

dfSample sizeMultiplier behaviour
12Largest multipliers; very heavy tails
45Visibly larger than z
1011Difference from z is not great
3031Visually near z, but t critical values remain larger

Verdict: increasing n narrows the interval twice over — through s/√n and through the multiplier. This is the one factor that improves precision without giving up confidence.

Navidi does not give a closed-form sample-size formula for the small-sample t interval; planning is usually done iteratively or with software.

4. Factor 3 — Population variability

s appears in the numerator, so the half-width is proportional to s: double s, double the width. A more variable population needs a larger sample to reach the same width.

This is also why sample-size planning requires an estimate of σ before n can be computed. In Session 31's bearing example, reducing σ was exactly the lever used to bring 95% of diameters inside a fixed specification.

Figure 5.4 notes that intervals from 100 samples differ mainly because each has a different x̄; the different s values matter far less for the width than the different centres matter for the location.

Verdict: higher variability widens the interval proportionally. Unlike the level and n, s is not chosen by the experimenter. It can be reduced only by reducing genuine variability — for example, by improving the measurement procedure.

5. Factor 4 — One-sided versus two-sided

One-sided bounds use subscript α, two-sided limits use α/2. Since tn−1,α < tn−1,α/2, a one-sided bound sits closer to x̄ at the same confidence level.

Numerical comparison

The 95% two-sided interval for microdrill lifetime is (10.79, 14.57). The 95% lower bound is 11.09.

Why 11.09 > 10.79: the two-sided interval can fail in two directions, so it allocates 2.5% to each tail. The lower bound never fails on the high side, so it spends the full 5% in the lower tail and can therefore be closer to x̄.

Verdict: if only one direction matters, a one-sided bound gives a tighter, more useful statement at the same level.

7. Reliability versus precision — the governing principle

Reliability
= confidence level 1 − α
Long-run coverage rate of the method.
Precision
= width of the interval
How tightly μ is pinned down.

At fixed n these pull against each other: buying reliability costs precision, and buying precision costs reliability. The only escape is a larger sample, which narrows the interval without touching the confidence level — and in a t interval, narrows it twice.

Design recipe
  1. Choose the confidence level (usually 95%) based on the cost of being wrong.
  2. Decide the half-width that would be practically useful.
  3. Estimate σ from pilot data or prior knowledge.
  4. Solve for n and round up. If n is impossible, either accept a wider interval or a lower level — those are the only honest options.

8. Key takeaways

  • The syllabus "margin of error" = tn−1,α/2·s/√n, the quantity after ±.
  • Higher confidence always widens the interval; 100% confidence requires an infinitely wide interval.
  • Larger n narrows the interval through both s/√n and df — the only free improvement.
  • Precision scales as 1/√n, so halving width costs about four times the data.
  • Population variability acts proportionally and is not under the experimenter's control.
  • One-sided bounds are tighter because the entire α is spent on the side that matters.

Unit 4 · Lesson L1Hypothesis tests·

Large-sample tests for a population mean

The five-step ritual, the meaning of a P-value, and the z test that mirrors Session 37's interval.

In one line: assume the default, measure how far the data stray from it in standard errors, and convert that distance into a P-value.

1. What a hypothesis test is

Definition

A hypothesis test is a formal procedure for deciding between two mutually exclusive statements about a population, using sample data. It produces a number between 0 and 1 measuring the strength of evidence against the default statement.

HypothesisRoleExamples
Null, H₀The default / status quo: no difference, no effect, no change. Always contains equality.μ = μ₀, μ ≤ μ₀, μ ≥ μ₀
Alternative, H₁The claim you want to establish — the research hypothesis.μ ≠ μ₀, μ > μ₀, μ < μ₀

They must be mutually exclusive and together cover all possibilities. Hypothesis tests and confidence intervals are two views of the same information: whenever a CI can be computed, a test can be performed, and vice versa (Lesson L3 makes this exact).

2. The five steps

Step 1 — State the hypotheses

Example: to show the mean is less than 100, write

H₀ : μ ≥ 100   vs   H₁ : μ < 100

The equality always belongs to H₀ (see Lesson L2 for why).

Step 2 — Assume H₀ is true

H₀ may contain many values, so we compute using the value closest to H₁ — the boundary value μ₀. This is the fairest, most conservative choice: it is the hardest value for the data to reject.

Under H₀, and approximating σ by s, the sample mean has the null distribution

x̄ ~ N( μ₀ , s/√n )
This distribution describes what x̄ would look like if H₀ were true.

Step 3 — Compute the test statistic

Large-sample z statistic
z = (x̄ − μ₀) / (s/√n)
The numerator is the observed distance from H₀; the denominator converts it into standard-error units. Valid for n ≥ 30. Large |z| means the data are far from H₀.

Step 4 — Compute the P-value

Definition — P-value

The probability, assuming H₀ is true, of observing a test statistic at least as far from H₀ as the one actually observed. It is an area under the null distribution.

Plain English: if H₀ were true, how unusual is what we saw?

AlternativeType of testP-value is…
H₁ : μ > μ₀Right-tailedArea to the right of z
H₁ : μ < μ₀Left-tailedArea to the left of z
H₁ : μ ≠ μ₀Two-tailed2 × the smaller tail area (both tails beyond ±|z|)
Two traps

The P-value is not the probability that H₀ is true. It is computed assuming H₀ is true.

A large P-value does not prove H₀. It only says the sample does not provide sufficient evidence to reject it.

Step 5 — Draw a conclusion

Decision rule at significance level α
P ≤ α ⇒ reject H₀   ·   P > α ⇒ do not reject H₀
α = 0.05 is the common convention, not a universal law. The z-score gives the distance; the P-value turns that distance into evidence.

3. Statistical versus practical significance

A result is statistically significant at level α when P ≤ α. That measures confidence that a difference exists — not whether the difference matters.

Why they differ

If s or the standard error is tiny (often because n is huge), even a microscopic difference can produce a small P-value. Always ask whether the observed difference is large enough to change a real decision.

4. Worked examples

Battery lifetime — right-tailed

A new battery is claimed to last more than 500 cycles. In 45 tests, x̄ = 512 and s = 38. Test at α = 0.05.

Show solution

Hypotheses. The claim is "more than 500", so the equality goes with H₀:

H₀ : μ ≤ 500  vs  H₁ : μ > 500,   μ₀ = 500

Statistic. s/√n = 38/√45 = 5.665.

z = (512 − 500)/5.665 = 12/5.665 = 2.12

P-value. Right-tailed: P = P(Z > 2.12) = 1 − 0.9830 = 0.017.

Conclusion. 0.017 ≤ 0.05, so reject H₀. There is sufficient evidence at the 5% level that the new batteries average more than 500 cycles.

Chlorine residual — two-tailed

A plant must keep mean chlorine at exactly 1.5 mg/L; both high and low are out of spec. In 50 readings, x̄ = 1.44 and s = 0.18. Test at α = 0.05.

Show solution

Hypotheses. Either direction is a problem, so:

H₀ : μ = 1.5  vs  H₁ : μ ≠ 1.5

Statistic. s/√n = 0.18/√50 = 0.02546.

z = (1.44 − 1.5)/0.02546 = −0.06/0.02546 = −2.36

P-value. Two-tailed: 2 × P(Z < −2.36) = 2 × 0.0091 = 0.0182.

Conclusion. 0.0182 ≤ 0.05, so reject H₀. There is statistically significant evidence that the true mean residual differs from 1.5 mg/L.

5. Key takeaways

  • H₀ is the default and owns the equality; H₁ is the claim you want evidence for.
  • Test against the boundary value of H₀ — the value closest to H₁.
  • z measures distance in standard errors; the P-value converts it to evidence.
  • The tail used depends entirely on the form of H₁.
  • Significance is not importance.

Unit 4 · Lesson L2Interpretation·

Drawing conclusions correctly

There are only two conclusions, neither of which is "H₀ is true" — plus how to choose hypotheses that answer the right question.

In one line: reject H₀ or fail to reject it. Never "accept" it, never "prove" it.

1. The only two possible conclusions

Conclusion A
H₀ is false — reject H₀
Conclusion B
H₀ is plausible — do not reject H₀
We can never conclude H₀ is true

"Do not reject" means only that the data are consistent enough with H₀ that we cannot rule it out. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

Example. If H₀ : μ ≥ 675 and x̄ = 673.2, we cannot say μ ≥ 675 is true — 673.2 is below 675. We can only say 673.2 is close enough that H₀ might still hold.

2. When to reject

Smaller P-values make H₀ less plausible. The common rule of thumb is reject when P ≤ 0.05, but there is no sharp scientific cutoff; 0.05 is a convention.

Statistical significance

If P ≤ α, the result is statistically significant at the 100α% level. Equivalently, "H₀ is rejected at level 100α%".

P ≤ 0.05 ⇒ significant at 5%; P ≤ 0.01 ⇒ significant at 1%.

The nesting rule

If a result is significant at α, it is automatically significant at every larger level, but not necessarily at smaller ones. With P = 0.03 it is significant at 10% and 5%, but not at 1% (since 0.03 > 0.01).

3. Choosing H₀ and H₁ to answer the right question

The hypotheses must be set up so that rejecting versus not rejecting leads to genuinely different actions.

Rule for one-tailed tests

The equality (=) always belongs with H₀.

  • Allowed: H₀ : μ ≤ μ₀ or H₀ : μ ≥ μ₀
  • Not allowed: H₀ : μ < μ₀ or H₀ : μ > μ₀

Reason: the null distribution needs one specific value of μ — the value in H₀ closest to H₁.

4. Significance is not practical importance

QuestionAnswered by
How strong is the evidence that a difference exists?The P-value
Is the difference large enough to matter?Domain judgement — not statistics

A difference is statistically significant when it is large relative to its standard error. A tiny difference with a tiny standard error can be highly significant and practically irrelevant.

5. Worked examples

Corrosion coating — one-tailed decision

Current coating has mean corrosion rate 0.5 mm/year. A cheaper coating will be adopted only if its mean rate is convincingly lower. In 40 specimens, x̄ = 0.46 and s = 0.09.

Show solution

(a) Hypotheses. The burden of proof is on the new coating being better, so that claim goes in H₁:

H₀ : μ ≥ 0.5  vs  H₁ : μ < 0.5

The equality must stay with H₀ because H₁ is the claim requiring convincing evidence. H₀ represents "no improvement, keep the current coating".

(b) Statistic. s/√n = 0.09/√40 = 0.01423.

z = (0.46 − 0.50)/0.01423 = −2.81  ⇒  P = P(Z < −2.81) = 0.0025

(c) Conclusion. 0.0025 ≤ 0.05 ⇒ reject H₀. There is strong evidence the new coating's mean rate is below 0.5 mm/year, so adopt it.

Interpretation. "Reject" means strong evidence that the new coating is genuinely better. "Do not reject" would have meant only that the evidence was insufficient — not that the coatings are equal.

GPS firmware — significance vs importance

Specified mean positioning error is 2.0 m. After a firmware update, 5,000 readings give x̄ = 1.97 m, s = 0.6 m. (a) Test H₀ : μ = 2.0 vs H₁ : μ ≠ 2.0 at α = 0.05. (b) Evaluate "significantly improves accuracy".

Show solution

(a) s/√n = 0.6/√5000 = 0.00849.

z = (1.97 − 2.00)/0.00849 = −3.54  ⇒  P = 2(0.0002) = 0.0004

0.0004 ≤ 0.05 ⇒ reject H₀. The true mean error differs significantly from 2.0 m.

(b) The result is statistically significant, but the improvement is only 3 cm (2.00 → 1.97 m). With n = 5,000 the standard error is minuscule, so even trivial differences become significant. For turn-by-turn navigation, 3 cm almost certainly does not justify a firmware rollout. Statistically significant ≠ practically significant.

6. Key takeaways

  • Two conclusions only: reject or fail to reject. Never "accept H₀".
  • Significance at α implies significance at every larger level, not at smaller ones.
  • Put the equality in H₀ and the burden-of-proof claim in H₁.
  • Report the P-value when possible; a bare reject/not-reject verdict hides information.
  • Always separate statistical significance from practical importance.

Unit 4 · Lesson L3Duality·

Confidence intervals and tests are the same statement

A CI is the set of all null values that would not be rejected. Understanding this removes half the memorisation.

In one line: a two-sided 95% CI contains exactly the μ₀ values for which a two-tailed test at α = 0.05 gives P > 0.05.

1. Two views of the same plausible values

ToolQuestion it answers
Hypothesis testIs this one specific value μ₀ plausible?
Confidence intervalWhat is the entire range of plausible values?

2. The two-sided case

Duality theorem

The values inside a two-sided level 100(1 − α)% confidence interval for μ are exactly those μ₀ for which the two-tailed test H₀ : μ = μ₀ vs H₁ : μ ≠ μ₀ has P > α.

The equivalence
μ₀ ∈ CI ⇔ P > α ⇔ do not reject H₀
μ₀ ∉ CI ⇔ P ≤ α ⇔ reject H₀

Mechanically: the CI is x̄ ± zα/2s/√n. A value μ₀ is inside exactly when |x̄ − μ₀|/(s/√n) ≤ zα/2, which is exactly the condition for a two-tailed z test not to reject. Same algebra, different presentation.

3. The one-sided case

A one-sided confidence bound corresponds to the boundary of a one-tailed test.

One-sided duality
  • If the 95% lower bound is L, then for any μ₀ > L, testing H₀ : μ ≤ μ₀ vs H₁ : μ > μ₀ gives P > 0.05.
  • If the 95% upper bound is U, then for any μ₀ < U, testing H₀ : μ ≥ μ₀ vs H₁ : μ < μ₀ gives P > 0.05.
Reading a bound as a test

An upper bound U is the exact tipping point: testing at μ₀ = U gives P = 0.05. Move μ₀ above U and the test statistic becomes more negative, so P < 0.05 (reject). Move μ₀ below U and P > 0.05 (do not reject).

4. Worked examples

Coating thickness — CI and test agree exactly

n = 60, x̄ = 10.2 mm, s = 1.5 mm. (a) Build a 95% CI. (b) Test H₀ : μ = μ₀ at the lower endpoint. (c) Predict the result for μ₀ = 10.0 and μ₀ = 9.5.

Show solution

(a) Standard error = 1.5/√60 = 0.1936. z0.025 = 1.96.

10.2 ± 1.96(0.1936) = 10.2 ± 0.3795 = (9.82, 10.58)

(b) With μ₀ = 9.82:

z = (10.2 − 9.82)/0.1936 = 1.96  ⇒  P = 2(1 − 0.9750) = 0.05

Exactly 0.05 — confirming the endpoint is where the decision flips.

(c) μ₀ = 10.0 lies inside (9.82, 10.58) ⇒ P > 0.05, do not reject. μ₀ = 9.5 lies outside, below the interval ⇒ P < 0.05, reject. No calculation needed: the interval already answers both.

Syringe fill — one-sided bound as a test

A 95% upper confidence bound for mean fill volume is U = 4.92 mL. For each μ₀, decide using only the bound: (a) H₀ : μ ≥ 5.05 vs H₁ : μ < 5.05; (b) H₀ : μ ≥ 4.85 vs H₁ : μ < 4.85.

Show solution

Rule: for an upper bound U, μ₀ > U ⇒ P < 0.05 (reject); μ₀ < U ⇒ P > 0.05 (do not reject).

(a) 5.05 > 4.92 ⇒ P < 0.05. Reject H₀: the data suggest the true mean is unlikely to be as high as 5.05 mL.

(b) 4.85 < 4.92 ⇒ P > 0.05. Do not reject: the data are consistent with a mean of at least 4.85 mL.

5. Why this matters in practice

  • A CI is more informative than a test: it shows which values are plausible and how precisely, not just a verdict on one value.
  • You can read a test result off a CI without recomputing anything — a major time-saver in exams.
  • The correspondence is exact only when the CI and test use the same level, the same distribution (z or t), and the same sidedness.
  • A 99% CI corresponds to a test at α = 0.01, not 0.05. Mixing levels is a classic error.

Unit 4 · Lesson L4t tests·

Small-sample t tests for μ

The same four steps as L1, with t in place of z — plus the one case where a small sample still uses z.

In one line: for n < 30 with unknown σ from an approximately normal population, replace z by t with n − 1 degrees of freedom.

1. Why a separate test is needed

The large-sample test replaces σ with s and uses the normal curve. That works when n is large because s is then close to σ. For small n, s may be far from σ, so the normal curve understates the uncertainty. If the population is approximately normal, the Student's t distribution accounts for that extra uncertainty.

Conditions for the t test
  1. The sample size is small (n < 30).
  2. The population is approximately normal.

If the population is clearly non-normal and n is small, neither z nor t is reliable; nonparametric methods would be needed.

2. The four steps

Step 1 — Hypotheses

Identical in form to the z statistic; only the reference distribution changes.

3. Why t instead of z?

  • Same symmetric bell shape, but heavier tails.
  • Heavier tails compensate for estimating σ by s in a small sample.
  • As n grows, t converges to z — which is why the choice barely matters for large n.

4. If σ is known, use z even for small n

Known σ, small sample
z = (x̄ − μ₀) / (σ/√n)
No estimation error remains, and for a normal population x̄ is exactly normal for any n. This is the only small-sample situation that uses z.

6. Worked examples

Tire tread life — right-tailed t test

A new compound is claimed to exceed 60,000 km. Seven tires give x̄ = 62,400 km, s = 2,100 km; tread life is approximately normal. Test at α = 0.05.

Show solution

Hypotheses. H₀ : μ ≤ 60,000 vs H₁ : μ > 60,000.

Statistic. n = 7, df = 6.

Critical value. Right-tailed at α = 0.05, df = 6 ⇒ t₆,₀.₀₅ = 1.943.

Conclusion. 3.02 > 1.943 ⇒ reject H₀. There is sufficient evidence at the 5% level that mean tread life exceeds 60,000 km.

Melting point — checking the assumption first

Six specimens melt at 661.2, 658.5, 662.0, 659.8, 660.5, 663.1 °C. (a) Is t appropriate? (b) Test H₀ : μ = 660 vs H₁ : μ ≠ 660 at α = 0.05.

Show solution

(a) A dotplot shows values fairly evenly spread with no outliers, no strong clustering and no obvious skew — consistent with approximate normality, so t is appropriate.

(b) x̄ = 660.85, s = 1.628, n = 6, df = 5.

t = (660.85 − 660)/(1.628/√6) = 0.85/0.6646 = 1.28

Two-tailed critical value: t₅,₀.₀₂₅ = 2.571. Since |1.28| < 2.571, do not reject H₀. The data are consistent with a mean melting point of 660 °C.

7. Key takeaways

  • Only the reference distribution changes between z and t tests; the four steps are identical.
  • Always compute df = n − 1 before reading the t table.
  • z vs t for a small sample depends on whether σ is known, not on n alone.
  • Check normality with a plot before using t — and never use t with outliers.

Unit 4 · Lesson L5ANOVA·

One-way ANOVA

Comparing more than two means without inflating the error rate — by comparing variation between groups to variation within groups.

In one line: if the group means are spread out far more than the noise inside the groups, F = MSbetween/MSwithin is large and H₀ is rejected.

1. Why not just run many t tests?

With 3 groups there are 3 pairwise comparisons; with 5 groups there are 10. Each test at α = 0.05 has a 5% false-positive chance, so the chance of at least one false positive balloons. ANOVA tests all means simultaneously at one controlled α.

2. Completely randomized experiments

  • Experimental units (specimens, plots, patients) are assigned to treatments.
  • If every possible assignment is equally likely, the design is a completely randomized experiment.
  • Randomisation prevents the design from favouring any treatment, so differences can be attributed to treatments rather than hidden bias.
  • If order could matter (instrument drift, warm-up), randomise the order too. If switching treatments is costly, run each treatment as a block but randomise the order of blocks.
  • Each treatment represents a population; its responses are a random sample. Population means are treatment means.

3. Notation

4. Hypotheses

H₀ : μ₁ = μ₂ = … = μI   vs   H₁ : two or more μi are different
H₁ does not say which means differ, or by how much. A significant F only justifies follow-up comparisons.

5. The idea: two sources of variation

ANOVA compares:

  1. Between treatments — how far the group means sit from the grand mean. Large ⇒ treatments differ.
  2. Within treatments — how far individual observations sit from their own group mean. This is pure random noise.
Treatment (between) sum of squares
SSbetween = Σi=1I Ji (x̄i − x̄)²
Each squared distance is weighted by sample size, so larger samples count more.
Error (within) sum of squares
SSwithin = Σi=1I Σj=1Ji (Xij − x̄i)² = Σi=1I (Ji − 1) si²
The terms (Xij − x̄i) are residuals; SSwithin is their sum of squares. It reflects only random variation and is unaffected by how far apart the treatment means are.
Total sum of squares — the ANOVA identity
SST = Σi Σj (Xij − x̄)² = SSbetween + SSwithin
Total variation = variation explained by treatments + unexplained variation.

6. Assumptions

The F test is valid when
  1. Each treatment population is normally distributed.
  2. All treatment populations have the same variance σ² (homoscedasticity).
  3. Observations are independent (guaranteed by random assignment).

7. Mean squares and the F statistic

Between mean square
MSbetween = SSbetween / (I − 1)

F = MSbetween / MSwithin,   df = (I − 1, N − I)
Under H₀, F ~ F(I − 1, N − I). The test is inherently right-tailed: only a large F is evidence against H₀.

Decision rule: P ≤ α ⇒ reject H₀; P > α ⇒ do not reject.

Unit 4 · Lesson L6Chi-square·

Chi-square goodness of fit

When there are more than two possible outcomes: does the whole set of probabilities match what was claimed?

In one line: compare observed counts with the counts expected under H₀, scaled so that every category contributes fairly.

1. From Bernoulli to multinomial

Earlier tests handled Bernoulli trials: two outcomes, success or failure, with probability p. A multinomial trial has k ≥ 2 possible outcomes with probabilities p₁, p₂, …, pk. Rolling a die is a multinomial trial with k = 6.

What the test does

The chi-square goodness-of-fit test checks whether a full set of multinomial probabilities equals a prespecified set p₀₁, …, p₀k.

2. Hypotheses

H₀ : p₁ = p₀₁, p₂ = p₀₂, …, pk = p₀k
H₁ : at least one pi ≠ p₀i
H₁ does not say which probability differs, only that the claimed distribution is not the whole truth.

3. Observed versus expected

QuantityMeaning
OiObserved count in category i after N trials
Ei = N · p₀iExpected count if H₀ were true

If H₀ holds, Oi should be close to Ei. If H₀ fails, at least some categories should deviate noticeably.

4. The test statistic

Chi-square goodness-of-fit statistic
χ² = Σi=1k (Oi − Ei)² / Ei
Each squared discrepancy is divided by its expected count. This standardisation prevents large categories from dominating and keeps small categories from being unfairly noisy.
χ² valueInterpretation
Near 0Observed ≈ expected — consistent with H₀
LargeObserved deviates strongly — evidence against H₀

5. The chi-square distribution and its condition

Null distribution

When all expected counts are sufficiently large, χ² approximately follows a chi-square distribution with k − 1 degrees of freedom, written χ²k−1.

Validity condition: every Ei ≥ 5. If any expected count is too small, combine categories before testing.

Degrees of freedom are k − 1 because the k counts must add to N, so once k − 1 of them are known the last is determined.

6. Steps

  1. State H₀ (with the p₀i) and H₁.
  2. Compute each Ei = N p₀i.
  3. Check all Ei ≥ 5; combine categories if needed.
  4. Compute χ².
  5. Set df = k − 1.
  6. Find the P-value as the area to the right of χ².
  7. P ≤ α ⇒ reject H₀; P > α ⇒ do not reject.
Reading the chi-square table

The table gives upper percentage points. To find the point cutting off the upper 5%, look in column α = 0.05 at the required df. Because exact values are usually not listed, report P as a range, e.g. "0.05 < P < 0.10", unless software is available.

Homogeneity and independence

Two tests with identical arithmetic and different sampling designs. The distinction is entirely about how the data were collected.

In one line: are the row distributions the same (homogeneity), or are the two classifications unrelated (independence)?

1. Contingency tables

  • Rows = categories of one criterion (machine, group, shift).
  • Columns = categories of a second criterion (outcome: too thin / OK / too thick).
  • Each cell holds the observed count in that row–column combination.
  • Marginal totals are the row totals and column totals.

2. The test for homogeneity

Several multinomial trials are conducted — one per row — each with the same set of possible outcomes. We ask whether all rows share the same outcome probabilities.

H₀ : for each column j, p₁ⱼ = p₂ⱼ = … = pIj
H₁ : for at least one column j, pij ≠ pkj for some i ≠ k
H₀ says the outcome distribution is the same regardless of group; H₁ says at least one group differs.
Expected count under homogeneity
Eij = (Oi· × O·j) / O
Under H₀ the best estimate of column j's probability is its overall proportion O·j/O; multiply by the row total.
Chi-square statistic and df
χ² = Σi=1I Σj=1J (Oij − Eij)² / Eij
df = (I − 1)(J − 1),   valid when all Eij ≥ 5
Arithmetic check

The expected row and column totals always equal the observed ones exactly. Use this to catch arithmetic slips before computing χ².

3. The test for independence

A single sample is drawn, and each item is classified on two criteria at once. Both row and column totals are random.

H₀ : the row and column classifications are independent
H₁ : the row and column classifications are not independent

Unit 4 · Lesson L8Decision theory·

Fixed-level tests and the two errors

When a decision cannot be deferred, fix α in advance, define a rejection region, and accept that either kind of mistake is possible.

In one line: P(Type I) ≤ α by construction; P(Type II) depends on the true value and can only be reduced by better design.

1. Why fix a level?

In ordinary testing we report a P-value and let the reader judge. But sometimes a firm decision is unavoidable — recalibrate the process or not, accept or return the shipment. A fixed-level test chooses the cutoff before seeing the data.

  1. Choose α, with 0 < α < 1, before looking at the data. This is the significance level.
  2. Compute the P-value in the usual way.
  3. P ≤ α ⇒ reject H₀; P > α ⇒ do not reject.

2. Critical points and rejection regions

Definitions

A critical point is the value of the test statistic that produces a P-value exactly equal to α — the dividing line on the statistic scale, mirroring α on the P-value scale.

The rejection region is the set of statistic values that lead to rejecting H₀. The critical point is included in it.

Test typeRejection regionCritical points
Right-tailed, H₁ : μ > μ₀Upper tail; statistic largeOne, at the α upper quantile
Left-tailed, H₁ : μ < μ₀Lower tail; statistic smallOne, at the α lower quantile
Two-tailed, H₁ : μ ≠ μ₀Both tails, α/2 eachTwo, symmetric

3. Type I and Type II errors

The four possible outcomes of a fixed-level decision.
Reality →
Decision ↓		H₀ trueH₀ false
Reject H₀Type I error (false positive)Correct ✓
Do not reject H₀Correct ✓Type II error (false negative)
Key result

If the significance level is α, then P(Type I error) ≤ α.

When H₀ specifies an exact value (μ = μ₀, on the boundary), the probability of landing in the rejection region is exactly α. When the true value lies strictly inside H₀'s region, the null distribution shifts away from the rejection region and the probability is even smaller.

4. Controlling both errors

The trade-off

Reducing α reduces Type I risk but generally increases Type II risk, for a fixed sample size. The two probabilities move in opposite directions.

Standard strategy:

  1. Choose α so the Type I error probability is acceptably small.
  2. Compute or estimate the Type II error probability at that α.
  3. If it is too large, redesign the experiment — most often by increasing n.

Type II is harder to control because it depends on the unknown true value of the parameter, not just on α.

5. Worked examples

Dissolved oxygen — rejection regions at different levels

A new filtration system should raise mean DO above 6 mg/L. σ = 0.5 is known, n = 50, and H₀ : μ ≤ 6 vs H₁ : μ > 6. (a) Find the 5% rejection region. (b) Is x̄ = 6.15 significant at 10%? (c) At 1%? (d) If the critical point is 6.10, what level is the test?

Show full solution

Standard error = σ/√n = 0.5/√50 = 0.0707.

(a) Right-tailed at α = 0.05 ⇒ z0.05 = 1.645.

critical x̄ = 6 + 1.645(0.0707) = 6.116  ⇒  reject if x̄ ≥ 6.116

(b) z = (6.15 − 6)/0.0707 = 2.12. At 10%, z0.10 = 1.282. Since 2.12 ≥ 1.282, reject.

(c) At 1%, z0.01 = 2.326. Since 2.12 < 2.326, do not reject.

The same data give different decisions at different α — which is exactly why reporting the P-value is more informative than a bare verdict.

(d) With critical point 6.10: z = (6.10 − 6)/0.0707 = 1.414. Level = P(Z ≥ 1.414) = 0.0786, about 7.9%. Moving the cutoff closer to μ₀ necessarily raises α.

Show solution

  • (b) True μ = 100, do not reject. H₀ true and not rejected ⇒ correct decision.

6. Key takeaways

  • Fix α before seeing data; that is what makes it a fixed-level test.
  • The critical point lives on the statistic scale; α lives on the P-value scale. They correspond one-to-one.
  • P(Type I) ≤ α always, with equality on the boundary of H₀.
  • Smaller α ⇒ larger Type II risk at fixed n. Increase n to improve both.
  • Type II probability depends on the true parameter value, so it is inherently harder to pin down.

Unit 4 · Lesson L9Power·

Power of a test

The probability of catching a real effect — and how to plan a study that has enough of it.

In one line: power = 1 − P(Type II) = the chance of rejecting H₀ when it is false, evaluated at one specific alternative value.

1. Definition

Power
Power = P(reject H₀ | H₀ is false) = 1 − P(Type II error)
A useful test needs small probabilities of both error types. α controls Type I directly; once α is fixed, power tells you whether Type II risk is acceptable.

2. When power is calculated

  • Before data collection — at the planning stage.
  • Purpose: check whether the proposed test is likely to detect a real effect before spending time and money.
  • There is little value in running an experiment with a poor chance of succeeding even when an effect exists.

3. Power requires a specific alternative

Power is not one number for a test. It depends on which particular value under H₁ is assumed true, because different values are separated from H₀ by different amounts.

True valueSeparation from H₀Power
Close to μ₀SmallLow — hard to distinguish
Far from μ₀LargeHigh — easy to detect

So power must always be stated as "the power to detect a difference of ___".

4. How to compute power

  1. Compute the rejection region exactly as in a fixed-level test, using the null distribution and α.
  2. Assume the specific alternative value μ₁ is true.
  3. Compute the probability that the statistic falls in that same rejection region under the alternative distribution.
One-tailed, large-sample mean
Power = P( x̄ in rejection region | μ = μ₁ )
The alternative distribution is the null distribution shifted to μ₁, with the same spread σ/√n. The farther μ₁ is from μ₀, the more of it lies beyond the critical point.

5. How large should power be?

There is no scientific cutoff. By convention, power above 0.80, sometimes 0.90, is considered acceptable. If power is too low, the experiment is unlikely to detect a real effect and may waste resources.

6. Increasing power and planning sample size

The usual fix is a larger sample. In planning, work backward: specify a target power and the smallest effect worth detecting, then solve for n.

Sample size for target power 1 − β (one-tailed mean test)
n = [ (zα + zβ) σ / (μ₁ − μ₀) ]²
zβ is the upper-tail z for β = 1 − power (e.g. z0.10 = 1.282 for 90% power). Always round up; the achieved power will then be slightly above target.

7. Worked examples

Fitness tracker — power at a specific alternative

Step-count error has σ = 1.2, n = 40. Test H₀ : μ ≥ 5 vs H₁ : μ < 5 at α = 0.05. (a) Find the rejection region. (b) Find power if the true mean is 4.5.

Show full solution

Standard error = 1.2/√40 = 0.1897.

(a) Left-tailed at 5% ⇒ z0.05 = 1.645.

critical x̄ = 5 − 1.645(0.1897) = 4.688  ⇒  reject if x̄ ≤ 4.688

(b) Under μ = 4.5:

z = (4.688 − 4.5)/0.1897 = 0.99
Power = P(Z ≤ 0.99) = 0.839

There is about an 84% chance of correctly detecting that the true mean has dropped to 4.5, with this n and α.

Manufacturing yield — required sample size

σ = 15, H₀ : μ ≤ 200 vs H₁ : μ > 200 at α = 0.05. Find n so that power is at least 0.90 to detect μ₁ = 206.

Show full solution

zα = z0.05 = 1.645; power 0.90 ⇒ β = 0.10 ⇒ zβ = 1.282; μ₁ − μ₀ = 6.

8. Power for other tests

The direct "shift the distribution" method is simplest for one-tailed, large-sample mean tests. It extends to two-tailed tests and proportions with more algebra. For t, F and chi-square tests, power is almost always computed with software.

9. Key takeaways

  • Power is the complement of Type II error probability.
  • It is always conditional on a specific alternative value.
  • Compute it before collecting data, to avoid a doomed experiment.
  • 0.80–0.90 is the conventional target; the only general way to raise it is more data.

Unit 4 · Lesson L10Design levers·

What changes the power

Four levers: α, n, the distance from H₀, and σ. Only two of them are genuinely yours to pull.

In one line: power rises with α, n and effect size, and falls with σ.

Summary — memorise this table.
FactorChangeEffect on powerCost or comment
Significance level αIncreaseIncreasesRaises Type I error risk too
Sample size nIncreaseIncreasesCosts data/time; the preferred lever
Distance |μ₁ − μ₀|IncreaseIncreasesNot controllable — a property of reality
Population σIncreaseDecreasesOnly reducible by improving measurement/process

1. Significance level α

Increasing α widens the rejection region: a larger slice of the null tail counts as "reject". A larger slice of the alternative distribution then falls inside it, so power rises.

The catch

A larger α also raises P(Type I error), which is capped at α. In practice α is fixed first (commonly 0.05) according to how much false-positive risk is tolerable, and power is evaluated afterward. α is rarely adjusted just to buy power.

2. Sample size n

Larger n shrinks the standard error σ/√n, making both the null and alternative distributions narrower and more concentrated around their centres. They overlap less, so less of the alternative distribution falls on the "do not reject" side of the critical point.

The practical lever

If a planned experiment lacks power, the standard remedy is more data, not a looser α. Sample-size calculation is done in reverse: fix target power and the smallest effect worth detecting, then solve for n.

3. Distance from the null value (effect size)

The farther the true value sits from μ₀, the more of the alternative distribution lies beyond the critical point. A test can have low power for a small effect even with a large sample, and high power for a large effect even with a modest one.

This is precisely why power has no single value: it must be quoted for a stated difference.

4. Population standard deviation σ

Larger σ widens both distributions and increases their overlap, so less of the alternative falls in the rejection region. Power falls.

σ is usually not under the experimenter's control — it belongs to the population and the measurement process. It can sometimes be reduced by improving measurement precision. More often, its effect is offset by increasing n, since σ/√n shrinks with n.

5. Putting the levers together

Design checklist
  1. Fix α from the tolerable Type I risk (usually 0.05).
  2. State the smallest effect that would matter practically — that fixes μ₁ − μ₀.
  3. Estimate σ from pilot data or prior knowledge.
  4. Choose a target power (usually 0.80 or 0.90) and solve for n.
  5. If n is infeasible, the honest options are: accept lower power, accept a larger detectable effect, or improve measurement precision.

6. Key takeaways

  • Power and Type II error are complements: Power = 1 − β.
  • Raising α buys power at the price of Type I risk.
  • Raising n buys power with no statistical price — only a practical one.
  • Bigger effects are easier to detect; that is not a property of the test.
  • High variability masks real effects; more data is the usual compensation.

"The probability is 95% that μ is in this interval."For a computed interval, say confidence, not probability. μ and the endpoints are fixed.
Using z for a small sample with unknown σ.Use t with n − 1 df, provided the population is approximately normal.
Using t when σ is known.Use z; there is no estimation penalty.
Using t with an outlier present.The method is invalid. Check a boxplot or dotplot first.
Rounding a sample size down.Always round up.
Using zα/2 for a one-sided bound.Use zα (or tn−1,α) — the whole α is in one tail.
Forgetting the √n in the standard error.SE = s/√n, never s alone.
Computing an interval from non-random data.Check for trends or cycles in time order; the method requires a random sample.

Hypothesis tests

MistakeCorrection
Putting the equality in H₁.The equality always belongs to H₀; H₁ is the claim requiring evidence.
Testing against the wrong μ₀.Use the value in H₀ closest to H₁ — the boundary.
Doubling the P-value for a one-tailed test.Double only for a two-tailed H₁.
Saying "H₀ is true" after a large P-value.Say "do not reject" or "insufficient evidence".
Interpreting the P-value as P(H₀ true).It is P(data at least this extreme | H₀ true).
Treating statistical significance as practical importance.Check the actual size of the effect, not just the P-value.
Using a t table without computing df.df = n − 1; wrong df gives the wrong critical value.
Reporting an exact P from a t table.Report a range unless software provides an exact value.

ANOVA

MistakeCorrection
Using df = N − 1 for both sources.Between: I − 1. Within: N − I. Total: N − 1.
Concluding which means differ from a significant F.F only says "some differ"; follow up with pairwise comparisons.
Ignoring unequal sample sizes.Weight each group mean by Ji in SSbetween and the grand mean.
Using ANOVA with unequal variances.The F test assumes a common σ²; check or use an alternative.
Reporting R² as if it proves causation.R² is the proportion of variation explained, nothing more.

Chi-square

MistakeCorrection
Using df = k for goodness of fit.df = k − 1.
Using df = IJ − 1 for a contingency table.df = (I − 1)(J − 1).
Checking observed counts against 5.The condition is on expected counts.
Using a two-tailed P-value.Chi-square tests are right-tailed: only large χ² is evidence.
Calling a fixed-row-total design "independence".Fixed row totals ⇒ homogeneity.

Errors and power

MistakeCorrection
Swapping Type I and Type II.Type I = false positive (reject a true H₀). Type II = false negative (fail to reject a false H₀).
Saying power is a fixed property of a test.Power depends on the specific alternative value.
Thinking a smaller α always improves a test.Smaller α raises Type II risk at fixed n.
Computing power after seeing the data.Power is a planning calculation, before data collection.
Using zα/2 in the power sample-size formula for a one-tailed test.Use zα.

Self-check & exam strategy

Rapid recall questions with answers, plus a timing plan for the paper.

1. Rapid recall

State the Central Limit Theorem and one consequence.

For a large random sample, x̄ is approximately N(μ, σ²/n) regardless of the population shape. Consequence: we can build z-based intervals and tests for μ without knowing the population distribution.

Why does s² divide by n − 1?

The n deviations around x̄ sum to zero, so only n − 1 are free. Dividing by n − 1 makes s² unbiased for σ².

Give the MSE decomposition and explain the trade-off.

MSE = Var(θ̂) + Bias². A slightly biased estimator with much lower variance can beat an unbiased but highly variable one.

What does 95% confidence actually mean?

The method covers μ in 95% of repeated samples. It is not the probability that a particular computed interval contains μ.

When do you use t instead of z?

Small sample, σ unknown, population approximately normal. If σ is known, use z even for small n.

What are the df for a t interval?

n − 1.

Why is a one-sided bound tighter than the corresponding two-sided limit?

It spends all of α in one tail instead of α/2 in each, so its multiplier is smaller and it sits closer to x̄.

State the P-value definition precisely.

The probability, assuming H₀ is true, of a test statistic at least as far from H₀ as the observed value.

What are the only two conclusions of a test?

Reject H₀, or do not reject H₀. Never "accept" or "prove" H₀.

How does a CI relate to a two-tailed test?

A level 100(1 − α)% CI contains exactly the μ₀ values for which the two-tailed test at α gives P > α.

What does a significant ANOVA F tell you — and not tell you?

It tells you at least one treatment mean differs. It does not identify which ones or by how much.

Give the df for each chi-square test.

Goodness of fit: k − 1. Homogeneity or independence: (I − 1)(J − 1).

State the chi-square validity condition.

All expected counts must be at least 5; combine categories otherwise.

Define Type I and Type II errors.

Type I: reject a true H₀ (false positive). Type II: fail to reject a false H₀ (false negative).

Define power and state the four factors that affect it.

Power = P(reject H₀ | H₀ false). It rises with α, n and effect size, and falls with σ.

Why is power not a single number?

It depends on the specific alternative value assumed true; different alternatives are separated from H₀ by different amounts.

3. Exam strategy

  1. Read the verb. "Estimate" ⇒ interval. "Test / is there evidence" ⇒ hypothesis test. "How many" ⇒ sample size. "Compare three groups" ⇒ ANOVA. "Counts / categories" ⇒ chi-square.
  2. Write the hypotheses before touching numbers, and put the equality in H₀. This earns method marks even if arithmetic slips.
  3. List given values with symbols (n, x̄, s, α, σ). It prevents mixing s and σ.
  4. State the distribution and df explicitly before looking up a table.
  5. Draw the curve and shade the tail. It takes ten seconds and prevents one-tailed/two-tailed errors.
  6. Keep 4 significant figures in intermediate steps; round only the final answer.
  7. Write the conclusion in context — mention the variable and units, not just "reject H₀".
  8. Check plausibility. A CI should contain x̄. A t multiplier should exceed the corresponding z. F and χ² must be non-negative.
