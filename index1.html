MODULE 01

Introduction to Entrepreneurship

What is Entrepreneurship?

DEFINITION Entrepreneurship is the process of acting upon ideas and opportunities to create value — turning a perceived problem or gap into a product, service, or venture that people are willing to pay for.

It is deliberately not defined narrowly as "starting a company" — value creation can happen inside an existing organisation too (this is called intrapreneurship).

Entrepreneur vs Entrepreneurial (mindset) vs Business Owner

Entrepreneur

Someone who identifies an opportunity, assembles resources they don't yet fully control, and takes on risk to create new value — usually through growth and innovation.

Business / Shop Owner

Runs a stable, often unchanging operation (e.g. the same shop for 20 years with no innovation). Manages an existing model rather than searching for a new, scalable one.

KEY POINTBeing "entrepreneurial" is a mindset, not a job title — an employee inside a large company can behave entrepreneurially (intrapreneurship); a shop owner repeating the same routine for years is not, by this definition, "acting entrepreneurially" even though they own a business.

SME vs Innovation-Driven Enterprise (IDE)

Not all new businesses are the same kind of venture. The course draws a sharp line between two types:

SME (Small & Medium Enterprise)
  • Local or regional market
  • Low risk, steady cash flow
  • Grows linearly (one store → a few more stores)
  • e.g. a local restaurant, a neighbourhood store
Innovation-Driven Enterprise (IDE)
  • Global market ambition
  • High risk, high-growth potential
  • Grows exponentially, backed by venture capital
  • e.g. Flipkart, Zomato, Stripe
KEY POINTAn IDE is classified as global, high-risk, with high-growth potential — this is the standard multiple-choice answer whenever "innovation-driven enterprise" is described.

The Invention Cycle & the Innovation Formula

Entrepreneurship converts an invention (a new idea, technology or method) into innovation (an invention that has actually created value in the market). An invention that never reaches anyone who needs it has not yet become an innovation.

Innovation Formula
Innovation = Invention × Commercialization
(An idea multiplied by zero commercialisation is still zero innovation.)

Entrepreneurial Myths & Traits

Common myths the course explicitly busts:

  • "Entrepreneurs are born, not made." — False. Entrepreneurial skills (spotting problems, tolerating risk, persistence) can be learned and practised.
  • "You need a completely original idea." — False. Most successful ventures are new combinations or better executions of existing ideas.
  • "Entrepreneurs are reckless risk-takers." — False. Good entrepreneurs manage and reduce risk methodically; they don't simply gamble.
KEY POINTA recurring exam-style question: "Entrepreneurial myths often suggest that entrepreneurs are ___" → the myth being tested is "born, not made." The correct, course-endorsed position is the opposite: the mindset is learnable through deliberate practice and self‑awareness.

What Is a "Startup"?

Steve Blank's definition
A startup is a temporary organization designed to search for a
repeatable and scalable business model.

Two words matter here: repeatable (the same sales/delivery process works for every new customer without being reinvented each time) and scalable (revenue can grow much faster than cost as the company grows).

OFFICIAL / GOVERNMENT DEFINITION (INDIA — DPIIT) An entity is recognised as an Indian "Startup" if, broadly: it is registered/incorporated for less than 10 years; turnover has not exceeded the prescribed limit; and — the point most tested — it is working towards innovation, development, or improvement of products/processes/services, or has scalable business potential with high potential for employment/wealth creation. In practice this is why the emphasis in class is on the entity showing a prototype or Minimum Viable Product (MVP) as evidence of genuine innovation rather than just a copied local business.

C.O.P. — A Simple Way to Start Looking

Before chasing "the perfect idea," the course suggests starting from three personal anchors:

CapabilitiesWhat can I actually do well?
+
OpportunitiesWhat gaps/problems do I notice?
+
PassionWhat do I care enough to stick with?
KEY POINTThe first step in becoming an entrepreneur, per the course, is neither "buying office space" nor "hiring a team" nor "designing a logo" — it is identifying a problem or an opportunity.

The India Startup Landscape

  • India is (as taught in this module) the 3rd largest startup ecosystem in the world, after the US and China.
  • Government schemes (Startup India, DPIIT recognition, tax benefits) exist to formalise and support this definition of a "startup."

Find Your Path — Ikigai

KEY POINTIkigai ("a reason for being") is a Japanese concept used to help founders find sustainable motivation. It denotes the intersection of passion and purpose — doing something that gives value and joy — rather than chasing money or trends alone.

Ikigai is traditionally drawn as the overlap of four circles:

CircleQuestion
What you lovePassion
What you're good atVocation/Skill
What the world needsMission
What you can be paid forProfession

For entrepreneurs, ikigai is discussed as the reason it's important to anchor a venture in something meaningful to the founder — because startups are long, difficult journeys and passion alone sustains people through the hard years.

MODULE 02

Mindset & Opportunity Seeking

The Entrepreneurial Mindset

A mindset is a set of beliefs that shapes how a person interprets and responds to situations. The course leans heavily on Carol Dweck's work on Growth vs Fixed mindsets:

Fixed Mindset
  • Believes intelligence/talent is static
  • Avoids challenges to protect self-image
  • Sees effort as a sign of weakness
  • Ignores or resents feedback
Growth Mindset
  • Believes abilities can be developed
  • Views challenges as opportunities for growth and learning
  • Persists through difficulty; effort is the path to mastery
  • Learns from criticism instead of avoiding it
KEY POINT"An entrepreneurial mindset can be developed through deliberate practice and self-awareness" — the course's answer is an unqualified yes: it is something anyone can learn through regular practice (not something reserved for people from "business families," and not something requiring an MBA).

A growth-mindset attribute that is repeatedly tested: learning from criticism and persisting through difficulties — the opposite of avoiding challenges, treating intelligence as fixed, or judging success only by outcomes and not effort.

Three Core Traits: Creativity, Teamwork, Adaptability

Creativity

Framed as a trainable skill (not innate talent) — practised through techniques like brainstorming, reframing problems, and deliberately exposing yourself to unrelated fields for cross-pollination of ideas.

Teamwork & Tuckman's Stages

Teams are described as passing through predictable stages as they mature:

FormingPoliteness, feeling out the group
→
StormingConflict over roles/ideas surfaces
→
NormingNorms and trust are established
→
PerformingHigh-functioning, productive team

Adaptability

The willingness to change plans, pivot the business model, or abandon a failing approach quickly when new evidence arrives — a direct antidote to "sunk cost" thinking.

Dealing with Failure

KEY POINTFailure is deliberately reframed: "Failure provides a valuable learning opportunity to build a better approach" — not something to be avoided at all costs, not proof that "successful entrepreneurs never fail," and not something that's acceptable only in the "early phases."

The course cites well-known founders who failed multiple times before succeeding, to normalise failure as data rather than a verdict on the founder's worth.

Problems Are an Entrepreneur's Best Friend, and the NABC Framework

KEY POINTThe module's central claim: "Every problem is a potential opportunity." Entrepreneurs are trained to seek out problems rather than avoid them, because a well-understood problem is the raw material for a venture.

NABC — a simple pitch/idea framework

N·A·B·C
Need — What real customer need exists?
Approach — What is our unique solution to it?
Benefit — What value/benefit does it create, and for whom?
Competition — How is this better than alternatives?
KEY POINTA core component of the NABC method that's frequently tested is identifying the "A" — a unique Approach/solution — the specific way the venture proposes to address the Need, as opposed to team strengths, investors, or "the most profitable market."
MODULE 03

Opportunity Identification & Ethics

Idea vs Opportunity

Idea

A concept or inspiration — interesting, but untested. May or may not have a market.

Opportunity

An idea that has been validated to have a real customer need and the potential to create value (someone will actually pay for it).

KEY POINTThe key distinguishing test: "An opportunity has customer need & value creation; an idea is just inspiration." Ideas do not always come only from technology, and opportunities do not always come only from "markets" in the abstract — the test is need + value, not the source.

Sources of Startup Opportunity

The course lists legitimate sources of opportunity:

  • Customers' needs (unmet or poorly served needs)
  • Technology-driven changes (a new technology makes something newly possible)
  • Social / market discontinuities (a shift in regulation, demographics, or behaviour opens a gap)
KEY POINT"Tax avoidance" is explicitly NOT listed as a legitimate source of startup opportunity — it's the standard wrong-answer trap in this topic.

The IDE Quadrant & Opportunity Attractiveness

Opportunities are often plotted on a 2×2 grid of Risk vs Growth potential to decide whether they suit an SME path or an IDE (venture-backed) path — reinforcing the M1 distinction: IDEs deliberately choose the high-risk / high-growth quadrant because only that combination can justify external, venture-style investment.

The TIPSC Filter

A checklist for screening whether a raw idea is actually a good opportunity worth pursuing:

T·I·P·S·C
Timely — Does it fit market/technology conditions right now?
Important — Does it address a real need or pain point?
Profitable — Can it realistically generate revenue and returns?
Sustainable — Does it have long-term viability, not just a quick win?
Context — Does it fit the economic, social, legal and technological environment?
KEY POINTIn the TIPSC filter, the letter "S" stands for Sustainable (not "Scalable" or "Solvable" or "Social" — those are common decoy options).

Opportunity in the Real World

Case — Airbnb Airbnb was created after its founders noticed that hotels in the city were sold out during a conference, while they had spare space in their own apartment — a social/market discontinuity (a demand spike hotels couldn't meet) turned into a scalable marketplace for spare space.
Case — Niche focus over head-on competition Sarah's AI personal-finance startup faced a saturating market. Rather than competing head-on, she narrowed to a niche — small-business owners — trading a larger addressable market for less competition and more specialised offerings. This illustrates that innovation/opportunity-sizing is a trade-off, not "no need for innovation."

Ethics for Startups

KEY POINTThe stated purpose of business ethics for a startup is "to build integrity for longevity" — not to maximise profits quickly, avoid taxes, or simply attract more investors. Ethical conduct is framed as a long-term survival strategy, not a constraint on growth.

Integrity is called out as a key ethical trait for founders — as opposed to ignoring small legal issues, cutting corners, or bypassing regulation for speed.

The "Downward Spiral" & Ethical Risk

KEY POINTA key ethical risk startups face: unethical decisions made under survival pressure — the intense pressure of a fast-paced, resource-constrained environment can push founders toward shortcuts they wouldn't otherwise take. This is different from ordinary business risks like running out of funding or launching early, which are operational risks, not ethical ones.

Obstacles to Ethical Decision-Making

The single biggest obstacle called out in this module is rationalizations — the internal stories people tell themselves ("everyone does it," "it's just this once," "the ends justify the means") that talk them into a decision they know is wrong. This is different from neutral operating concepts like transparency, ownership, or fair pricing, which are ethical goals, not obstacles.

Data Ethics

The 5 Aspects of Data Ethics
Ownership — who owns the data being collected?
Transparency — is it clear to users how data is used?
Privacy — is personal data protected appropriately?
Intention — why is the data really being collected/used?
Outcomes — what real-world effects does its use create?
KEY POINTData Ethics emphasises Ownership, Transparency, Privacy, Intention, Outcomes. "Speed" is explicitly not one of the five aspects — it's the standard decoy answer in this topic.
MODULE 04

Building & Leading Teams

Why Startups Fail — and What Makes a Team Work

KEY POINTPer CB Insights' research into startup post-mortems, "not the right team" is consistently one of the top reasons startups fail — ranking alongside "no market need" and "ran out of cash." It's a bigger and more common killer than "poor marketing," "weak technology," or "slow hiring."

Characteristics of an Effective Team

  • Good communication
  • Decisions by consensus
  • Continuous learning and sharing of information
  • Trust and shared beliefs
KEY POINTWithholding information is explicitly listed as the opposite of an effective team's behaviour — it is NOT a characteristic of a good team, it actively damages it.

The "Perfect Team" Myth (Y Combinator-style framing)

Teams that function well do not chase an unrealistic "perfect team" that never disagrees. Instead, a team trying to build a genuinely strong team does not avoid having hard conversations — they actively make plans, define goals/process, and communicate honestly, precisely by confronting difficult topics rather than dodging them.

Diversity, Inclusion & the Founding Team

KEY POINT — VERNA MYERS Diversity consultant Verna Myers' famous distinction:
Diversity is "being invited to the party."
Inclusion is "being asked to dance."
(i.e., simply having diverse people present isn't enough — they must be genuinely engaged and given a voice.)

Opinion diversity in entrepreneurial teams is best kept at a moderate level — enough differing perspectives to challenge groupthink and stress-test ideas, but not so much dissent that the team can't converge on decisions and act.

Founding Team Roles

A founding team is often described using three functional archetypes:

RoleFocus
VisionarySets direction, generates the big idea, sells the future
Builder / HackerThe engineer/architect who actually builds the product or solution
Seller / HustlerDrives sales, customers, fundraising, and business development
KEY POINT"I am an Engineer or an Architect who basically builds the Product/Solution — who am I?" → Hacker (the technical builder role, as distinct from the Hustler/business role or the Visionary).

The CEO's role in a founding team is often described as "creating comfort in chaos" — providing stability, decisiveness and direction while the venture is inherently uncertain — rather than simply "generating ideas," "handling finances," or "managing customers" (which are other roles' jobs).

Leadership, DISC & Emotional Intelligence

DISC Personality Assessment

D · I · S · C
Dominance — direct, results-driven
Influence — outgoing, persuasive, enthusiastic
Steadiness (Stabilize) — patient, dependable, team-oriented
Cautious (Conscientious) — analytical, detail-oriented, careful

DISC is used to help teammates understand each other's default working styles, reducing friction that would otherwise be mistaken for "personality clashes."

Communication & Trust

KEY POINTAmong all communication channels, face-to-face communication is considered the most valuable for high-performing teams — richer than email, WhatsApp, or Slack/Teams messages, because it carries tone, body language, and immediate feedback.

Case studies in this module repeatedly show that lack of trust and poor communication — not lack of funding, technical skill, or product-market fit — is usually the "most critical" issue a struggling founding team needs to fix first. Where a technically strong co-founder creates conflict through poor communication, the recommended fix is coaching and feedback to improve collaboration — not ignoring it, replacing them outright, or promoting them to CEO to "leverage authority."

MODULE 05

Human-Centred Innovation

Two complementary toolkits for building the right thing: Design Thinking (understanding the problem) and Effectuation (acting under uncertainty with limited resources).

Design Thinking

ORIGIN STORYOne of the earliest and most famous Design Thinking projects at the innovation firm IDEO was the redesign of Apple's first mouse in the early 1980s — turning an expensive, unreliable lab device into an affordable, reliable consumer product. This case is the standard answer whenever the "earliest Design Thinking process at IDEO" is asked about (not the Aadhaar card, Tupperware, or the Logitech mouse).

The Design Thinking Stages

EmpathizeUnderstand users deeply
→
DefineFrame the real problem
→
IdeateGenerate many solutions
→
PrototypeBuild something testable
→
TestGet real user feedback
  • Empathize — e.g. a team spending weeks interviewing and observing commuters to understand daily frustrations, before proposing any solution, is squarely in the Empathize stage.
  • Ideate — brainstorming 50+ possible solutions without judging any of them is the hallmark of the Ideate stage (judgment is deliberately suspended here).
  • Test / Iteration — testing an early prototype with real users, gathering feedback, and redesigning features accordingly demonstrates the entrepreneurial concept of iteration (build → test → learn → rebuild), not "causal logic" or "market analysis" alone.
KEY POINTDesign Thinking is not limited to tech ventures — the claim "Design Thinking can be used only in Tech ventures" is False. It applies to any human-centred problem: healthcare, education, government services, physical products, etc.

Core principles of Design Thinking: Empathy, Ideation, Experimentation. Note that "Profitability" is deliberately NOT one of its core principles — Design Thinking is about desirability/usability first; profitability is assessed separately (see DVF below).

Why Design Thinking for a New Venture?

KEY POINTThe best reason to use Design Thinking when starting a venture is that it helps you understand real problems — not because "the course taught it" or because you simply "want to use something new," and not primarily to "find funding" (that's a downstream benefit at best).

Desirability–Viability–Feasibility (DVF)

DVF Lens
Desirability — Do people actually want this?
Viability — Can we make money doing it?
Feasibility — Can we actually build/deliver it?
Worked example — the chai cart A chai vendor's blend is loved by residents and there's good demand, yet the venture keeps losing money. Why? Because the cost of producing and delivering each cup is higher than what can be earned per cup — a Viability problem, even though Desirability is clearly fine.
Worked example — the unbuildable idea A team finds a widely-felt problem and confirms customers will pay for a fix — but the technology needed to build the solution simply doesn't exist yet. This is a Feasibility problem: Desirability and Viability look fine, but the "can we build it?" question fails.

Effectuation

Effectuation is a decision-making logic for acting under high uncertainty with limited resources — the opposite of causal logic, which starts from a fixed goal and works backward to plan the "optimal" path (typically requiring predictable markets and large upfront resources).

Causal Logic
  • Start with a defined goal
  • Predict the future, then plan
  • Gather resources to fit the plan
  • Works well in stable, known markets
Effectual Logic
  • Start with your available means
  • Control what you can, rather than predict
  • Let the goal emerge/evolve from what's possible
  • Works well in uncertain, new markets — most startups

The Five Effectuation Principles

Effectuation's 5 principles
1. Bird-in-Hand — start with who you are, what you know, whom you know
2. Affordable Loss — risk only what you can afford to lose
3. Crazy Quilt — build partnerships with self-selected stakeholders
4. Lemonade Principle — treat surprises as resources, not threats
5. Pilot-in-the-Plane — you shape the future by acting, not by predicting it
KEY POINT — BIRD-IN-HAND Two of the exam-style scenarios in this course both point to the same principle:
• A founder with very little funding builds a simple prototype using existing skills, works with friends, and tests it with a small customer group — instead of waiting for investors.
• A founder starts by asking: "What skills do I already have? What knowledge can I leverage? Who in my network can help me?"
Both are textbook Bird-in-Hand — starting a venture with the means already at hand (identity, knowledge, network), rather than a predetermined goal requiring outside resources first.
KEY POINTAt a high level, "Effectuation is a process to start with affordable steps" — not to procure startup funding, practice public speaking, or price a product. Its essence is incremental, resource-constrained action.
